I. PHYSICS AND INSTRUMENTATION
1.1 Basic Principle of US
Sound is a type of energy that creates mechanical vibrations in the molecules of a substance. For sound to
move from one place to another, it needs a material that contains molecules—so it cannot pass through a
vacuum.
Ultrasound consists of high-frequency sound waves that interact with body tissues. The speed at which
these waves travel depends on tissue density, known as acoustic impedance. Denser tissues, like
bone, have higher impedance, causing ultrasound to travel faster through them compared to less dense
tissues like fat.
When ultrasound waves hit a boundary between two tissues with different acoustic impedances, a
portion of the sound is reflected back to the transducer. The greater the difference in impedance, the
stronger the reflection.

Sound is a form of energy that moves through matter in waves.
It travels by causing particles in the material to compress and expand.
The type of material affects the wave’s speed and direction.
Ultrasound (U/S) probes send and receive these sound waves to produce images.
Ultrasound uses longitudinal waves (vibrations move in the same direction as the wave).
X-rays use transverse waves (vibrations move at a right angle to the wave direction).

1.2 How to produce Ultrasound wave
• The transducer contains piezoelectric crystals (or elements).
• These crystals create ultrasound pulses.
• They work by converting electrical energy into mechanical (ultrasound) waves, and can also convert
mechanical waves back into electrical signals.
• This two-way conversion process allows both sending and receiving of ultrasound signals.

An ultrasound wave is generated when an electric current is sent through a group of piezoelectric
crystals. This electric signal makes the crystals change shape, causing them to vibrate and create
sound waves.

Amplitude: This is the height of the wave. A bigger amplitude means more
energy and a “louder” sound. If you increase the power of the ultrasound, both
the amplitude and intensity go up.
Attenuation: As sound travels through the body, it loses energy, which causes it to get weaker. This
process is called attenuation. It happens more when the sound frequency is high or when it travels farther.
The main causes are absorption, reflection, and scattering.
Contact Coupling: This refers to the gel or liquid used between the transducer and the skin. It removes
air so the sound waves can pass into the body properly.
Diffuse Reflectors (Scatterers): These are surfaces that bounce sound waves in many directions. The
brightness of the image doesn’t depend on the angle of the sound hitting it.
Dynamic Range: This controls how many shades of gray you see on the screen. Lowering the dynamic
range removes faint echoes and makes the image look sharper with more contrast.
Echo: An echo is a sound wave that bounces back from tissue and is picked up by the ultrasound probe.
This usually happens when the wave hits the tissue at a 90-degree angle.

Frequency: The number of cycles of acoustic waves per second.

• Audible sound has a frequency between 20 and 20,000 Hz.
• Ultrasound has a frequency greater than 20,000 Hz.
• Diagnostic ultrasound has a frequency of 2–20 MHz.
Gain: The degree of amplification of the returning echo.
• A way to overcome ultrasound attenuation is time gain compensation (TGC), in which signal gain is increased
as time passes from the emitted wave pulse.
• This correction makes equally echogenic tissues appear the same even if they are located at different depths.
• If overall gain is set too high, it amplifies noise within the image and reduces both spatial and contrast
resolution.
• If overall gain is set too low, the image becomes too dark and detail is lost.

Propagation Speed:
• The speed depends on tissue density and is lowest in gases, higher in liquids, and highest in solids.
• The average speed of sound in soft tissue is 1,540 m/s.
• Propagation velocity is increased by increasing stiffness and reduced by increasing density.
Examples:
• Bone: 4,080 m/sec
• Air: 330 m/sec
• Soft tissue average: 1,540 m/sec
Reflection:
• Occurs when two large structures have significantly different acoustic impedance (such as an organ
boundary).
• Happens when a sound wave strikes an object larger than the wavelength.
• Interfaces not perpendicular to the beam will not be visualized during scanning because the reflected
echoes will not be received by the transducer.
Refraction:
• Occurs when the beam encounters an interface between two different tissues at an oblique angle.
• If the angle of incidence is 90°, no refraction will occur.

Scattering:
• Occurs when a sound wave strikes an object equal to or smaller than the wavelength.
• Directly related to the frequency of the wave.
Wavelength:
• The length of one cycle, usually measured in mm.
• As frequency increases, wavelength becomes shorter.
Transducer Backing Material:
• Placed behind the crystals to dampen vibrations and shorten the pulse duration.
Beam Shape:
• The beam is three-dimensional and symmetrical around its central axis.
The ultrasound beam can be subdivided into two regions:
1. Near field (Fresnel zone)
2. Far field (Fraunhofer zone)
• Increasing the frequency results in a longer near field.
• A narrow crystal diameter produces a narrower beam in the near field.

Resolution
Resolution refers to the ability of an imaging system to distinguish between structures, images, or events
and display them as separate entities.

1. Spatial Resolution – Resolution in Space
This is the ability to display two structures located close together as separate images. It depends on
the wavelength.
For example, a 5 MHz ultrasound beam has an approximate wavelength of 0.3 mm, so it is not
possible to resolve objects smaller than 0.3 mm.
Spatial resolution is divided into two components:
• Axial Resolution – Resolution along the screen (depth). Higher
frequencies produce shorter pulse lengths, resulting in better axial
resolution. However, using a large field of view (FOV) can reduce axial
resolution.
• Lateral Resolution – Resolution across the screen. It depends on
beam width; a narrow beam width provides better spatial
resolution. Two structures can only be resolved as separate
images if the distance between them is greater than the beam
width. The best lateral resolution is achieved at the focal zone.

2. Contrast Resolution – Resolution of Gray Shades
Contrast resolution is the ability of the imaging system to distinguish between body tissues and
display them as different shades of gray.
Controls that affect contrast (and therefore contrast resolution):
• Gain (amplification)
• Time-Gain Compensation (TGC)
• Post-processing and pre-processing options
• Harmonic imaging – improves contrast
3. Temporal Resolution – Resolution in Time
Temporal resolution is the ability of the imaging system to display events occurring at different times as
separate images.
It is determined by the frame rate (the number of images displayed per second).
Tip for optimizing resolution:
Select the highest frequency that still provides adequate penetration.

4. Ultrasound Artifact
Ultrasound Artifact
An artifact is an alteration in the ultrasonographic image that does not represent an actual depiction of
the examined area.
Artifacts may result from:
• Technical imaging errors (operator-dependent)
• Ultrasound wave interaction with the human body
Awareness, identification, and understanding of the basic mechanisms behind ultrasound artifacts are
essential for reducing the risk of misdiagnosis.
1. Acoustic enhancement artifact
Appearance A low attenuation region with increased echo amplitude
(Figure A).
Physics:
This occurs due to the low attenuation of the ultrasound beam when
passing through fluid, compared to the greater attenuation in the
adjacent, stronger tissue. As a result, no echoes are reflected in that
area.

2. Mirror Artifact
Appearance as a duplicated area with a high acoustic mismatch (Figure A).
Physics:
Mirror artifact is a type of primary ultrasound beam path artifact caused by the
presence of a highly reflective surface. The returned echoes are not reflected
directly back to the transducer; instead, they are reflected to another surface
before returning.
As a result, the image is duplicated — the real image from the original
beam and the mirror image from the return echoes are both displayed
(Figure B).
This artifact usually occurs when there is a large acoustic mismatch, such
as at a fluid–air interface.
Prevention:
Changing the patient’s position and adjusting the probe angle are recommended methods to eliminate
its occurrence. It arises due to specular reflection of the beam at a large, smooth interface.

3. Acoustic Shadowing Artifact
Appearance:
A low-amplitude echo area (hypoechoic or anechoic) located behind a tissue
that significantly attenuates the ultrasound beam (Figure A).
Physics:
This artifact is caused by heavy attenuation of the ultrasound beam at an interface
that transmits minimal sound. The attenuation may occur due to (Figure B):
• Absorption of the sound waves.
• Reflection of the sound waves.
Acoustic shadowing typically occurs at interfaces with large acoustic
incompatibility, such as:
• Gas and soft tissue – where beams are almost completely attenuated by
reflection
• Bone or calculus – where attenuation results from approximately 40% beam

reflection and 60% absorption by bone
Diagnostic Clues:
• If the shadowing contains reverberation (ringdown) echoes, it is likely caused by gas.
• If the shadowing is a clear, clean shadow without ringdown echoes, it is likely caused by a calculus.

Appearance:
A series of closely spaced, small echogenic bands appearing on the

• Delayed echoes are produced.
• Ultrasound amplitude is reduced as the echo width decreases (Figure B).
Common causes include the ultrasound beam passing through:
• Metal objects (e.g., needles, surgical instruments)

5. Biological Effects Of Ultrasound
Ultrasound produces biological effects by two tissue interactions: heating and cavitation.
• Heating: Caused by the mechanical friction of tissue movement during the passage of the ultrasonic
wave.
• Cavitation: The production and collapse of small bubbles in the inter- and intracellular tissue fluid.

Intensity
• The rate at which energy passes through a unit area.
• Important in discussing bioeffects and safety.
• Average intensity = power of the ultrasound beam (W) ÷ cross-sectional area of the beam (cm²).
• The maximum intensity occurs at the focus (narrowest part of the beam).
There are two distinct mechanisms that can cause biological effects on tissue when exposed to
ultrasound:
1. Thermal (Heating)
As the ultrasound beam travels through tissue, some energy is lost through absorption and converted
into heat, causing a temperature rise.
Depends on:
• Attenuation coefficient of the tissue
• Operating frequency
• Power/intensity of the ultrasound beam
• Length of operating time (exposure time)
• The thermal effect is highest in tissues with a high absorption coefficient (e.g., bone).
• It is lowest where there is little absorption (e.g., amniotic fluid/water).
Thermal Index (TI): The ratio of the acoustic power produced by the transducer (W) to the power
required to raise tissue temperature by 1°C (W/°C).

2. Non-Thermal Effects (Cavitation)
Ultrasound produces an oscillating pressure wave that propagates through tissues, which can cause
micro-bubbles.
• Increased by high intensity and low frequency.
Forms of cavitation:
1. Stable Cavitation (Non-Inertial) – Generally regarded as safe.
2. Unstable Cavitation (Transient/Inertial) – Has the greatest potential to damage tissue.
The use of contrast agents (stabilized gas bubbles) increases the probability of cavitation.

Simple terminology definitions
Velocity is the rate at which a sound wave moves through a
medium.
It does not depend on the wave’s frequency, but mainly on
the medium’s physical properties — specifically its
density and stiffness.

Factors Affecting Propagation
The speed at which sound travels through a material is determined by its stiffness and density.
• Stiffness (Bulk Modulus): Even a small increase in stiffness causes a large increase in sound velocity.
• Density: Higher density slightly reduces the speed of sound.
• Compressibility: This is the opposite of stiffness — greater compressibility leads to a slower sound
propagation speed.
Stand-off pads
These are used to increase the distance between the ultrasound probe and a lesion that is located very
close to the skin surface. They help position the area of interest within the probe’s focal zone, reduce or
eliminate reverberation artifacts, and improve overall image quality.

6. Doppler Ultrasound
A Doppler ultrasound is a non-invasive test used to investigate movement, particularly to evaluate
blood flow in arteries and veins.
Uses / Diagnostic Applications
Doppler ultrasound can help diagnose:
• Heart valve defects and congenital heart disease
• Blocked artery (arterial occlusion)
• Narrowing (stenosis) of an artery
• Movement of the cardiac wall
• Blood clots (deep vein thrombosis)
• Varicose veins (venous insufficiency)
• Arteriovenous malformations
Principle
The Doppler effect is utilized in ultrasound to detect blood flow by analyzing the relative frequency
shifts of the received echoes caused by the movement of red blood cells.

Another Factors:
1. Higher blood flow velocities create larger Doppler shifts, and lower velocities produce smaller shifts.
2. The smaller the angle between the Doppler beam and the blood vessel, the stronger the Doppler

Note:
In most real-world clinical cases, it’s not practical to have an insonation angle of 0 degrees (where the
ultrasound beam is perfectly aligned with blood flow). However, angle correction can be applied to get
an accurate velocity measurement.
• Angle correction works well and is considered reliable when the insonation angle is less than 60
degrees.
• When the angle exceeds 60 degrees, velocity calculations can have errors up to 20–30%.

Remember:
• The maximum Doppler shift and the true velocity occur when the ultrasound beam is parallel to
the blood flow (0 degrees).
• This is because the Doppler shift depends on the cosine of the angle between the beam and flow,
and the cosine of 0 degrees is 1 (meaning full shift).
• When the blood flow is perpendicular to the beam (90 degrees), no Doppler shift is detected since
the cosine of 90 degrees is 0.

Factors for Optimizing Doppler Imaging:
1. Gain:
• Increasing gain too much causes noise and false flow signals in areas without vessels (called “blooming”).
• Too low gain hides real blood flow.
• Best practice: raise gain until you see some artifacts in nonvascular areas, then lower it a bit for a clear
image.
2. PRF (Pulse Repetition Frequency) / Scale:
• PRF controls how many ultrasound pulses are sent per second.
• Higher PRF lets you measure higher blood flow velocities.
• If blood flow velocity exceeds the PRF limit, aliasing happens—this distorts velocity and direction
info in color and pulsed wave Doppler.
• Increasing PRF reduces aliasing but makes it harder to detect slow flow.
• Lowering PRF increases sensitivity to low velocities but risks aliasing if flow is fast.
3. Wall Filter:
• Removes low-frequency signals caused by movement of vessel walls or nearby tissues, reducing noise.
• Setting it too high can accidentally filter out real slow blood flow, so it needs careful adjustment.

4. Sample Volume Size:
• In color Doppler, the “sample box” defines the area examined. Larger boxes lower frame rate and
temporal resolution, so keep the box just big enough.
• In pulsed wave Doppler, the sample volume (or “gate”) should be about two-thirds the width of the
vessel to focus on faster central flow and reduce artifacts like spectral broadening.
5. Doppler Angle:
• In pulsed wave Doppler, adjust the Doppler angle to less than 60 degrees for accurate velocity
measurement.
• Small angle changes below 60 degrees cause minimal velocity errors.
• Angle correction is done by aligning a marker on the Doppler gate with the vessel’s long axis.
