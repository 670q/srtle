Lateral positions
A true lateral position is always 90° to a true AP (front-to-back)
or PA (back-to-front) view.

Decubitus positions
The patient lies on a flat (horizontal) surface, and the X-ray beam is
always directed horizontally.
This position is important for showing air-fluid levels or free air inside
body spaces like the chest or abdomen, because air naturally rises to
the top of the cavity.
Decubitus views are usually done when the patient is unable to stand
or sit upright.

Proximal vs. Distal
• Proximal means closer to the starting point or origin (like where a
limb connects to the body).
• Distal means farther away from the starting point or origin.

Cephalad vs. Caudad
Cephalad means toward the head end of the body,
while caudad means away from the head end or
toward the feet.
A cephalad angle refers to any angulation directed
toward the head. The term "cephalad" or "cephalic"
literally means "head" or "toward the head."
A caudad angle refers to any angulation directed
toward the feet or away from the head. "Caudad" or
"caudal" comes from cauda, meaning "tail."

1.3 General Principles for Determining Positioning

Routine

• The first general rule in radiology is that most exams need at least two images taken at 90
degrees from each other (like front and side views).
• Some exceptions—where one image is usually
enough—include:
• AP mobile chest X-ray
• AP abdomen (KUB: kidneys, ureters, bladder)
• AP pelvis
• The second rule applies to imaging of joints in the skeletal system.
• In these cases, at least three views are needed for proper assessment:
• AP or PA view
• Lateral view
• Oblique view

Correct Use of Grids
If a grid is used incorrectly during an X-ray, it can cause part or all of the image to appear too light — this is
called grid cutoff. It reduces the image's optical density and can happen for several reasons, including:
• Off-center grid – The X-ray beam isn’t lined up properly with the center of the grid.
• Off-level grid – The grid is tilted or angled instead of lying flat.
• Off-focus grid – The grid is used at the wrong distance from the X-ray tube.
• Upside-down grid – The grid is placed the wrong way, with the back side facing the tube.
Best Ways to Reduce Radiation Exposure
• Time – Keep the amount of time the radiation is on as short as possible.
• Distance – Stay as far away from the radiation source as you can (this applies to both staff and patients).
• Shielding – Always use protective shields (like lead aprons) to protect both the patient and the healthcare
team.

RADIATION UNITS
• Exposure:
This measures how much ionization (charge) is created in the air by X-rays.
It’s measured in roentgens (R)or coulombs per kilogram (C/kg). It’s used to describe things like how strong the
X-ray beam is, how much exposure the patient receives, or how much scatter radiation is present.

RADIATION UNITS
• Air Kerma:
This shows how much energy is transferred to air by the X-ray photons. It’s measured in grays (Gy) or
rads, and has replaced exposure as the preferred measurement for things like patient dose or
equipment output
• Absorbed Dose:
This tells us how much radiation energy is absorbed by tissue.
It’s measured in grays (Gy). Even if the dose is the same, different types of radiation can cause
different levels of damage.
• Equivalent Dose:
This is measured in sieverts (Sv) or rem, and it considers the type of radiation to estimate the
potential risk. It's calculated by multiplying the absorbed dose by a radiation weighting factor. In
radiography, this factor is always 1, because we mainly use X-rays.

Personnel Monitoring
Personnel monitoring means tracking how much radiation a worker is
exposed to on the job.
• A dosimeter (radiation badge) is worn to measure this exposure.
• During general radiography, it’s typically worn at the chest or waist level.
• For those working with fluoroscopy, most of the radiation reaches the head and neck, while the area
under the lead apron gets very little exposure.
• That’s why, during fluoroscopic procedures, the dosimeter should be worn on the collar, outside the
lead apron or thyroid shield.

Radiation Protection Practices During Fluoroscopy
Everyone involved in fluoroscopic procedures must wear a lead apron for protection.
An apron with 0.5 mm lead equivalent is recommended, as it can reduce radiation exposure by up
to 50 times within the diagnostic X-ray energy range.
Using intermittent fluoroscopy can greatly reduce the total time the X-ray beam is on.
This method is even more effective when used with the last image hold feature.
Modern fluoroscopy machines can keep the last image displayed on the screen even after the X-ray is
turned off, allowing the physician to review the most recent image without extra radiation exposure.
Intermittent fluoroscopy is a technique in which, instead of continuous image acquisition, the operator
obtains intermittent image sets—typically in groups of three—during key procedural steps such as needle
insertion, advancement, and manipulation.
This modern approach can potentially reduce both the procedure time and the patient's radiation
exposure.

2. Thoracic Cavity (Chest)
Degree of Inspiration
The best way to check if the patient took a deep enough breath during a chest X-ray
is to look at how low the diaphragm has moved.
You do this by counting the number of posterior ribs visible above the diaphragm
in the lung area.
For an average adult, a good PA chest X-ray should show at least 10 posterior ribs.
Breathing Instructions
Chest X-rays should always be taken during full inspiration, so the lungs appear fully expanded.
INSPIRAION AND Expiration
Although chest X-rays are usually taken on full inspiration, there are
some cases where both inspiration and expiration views are
needed for comparison. This is done to help identify certain
conditions, such as:
• A small pneumothorax (air trapped in the pleural space), or
• Diaphragm problems, like if it’s not moving normally or appears
fixed in place.

Chest X-rays should be done with the patient standing or sitting upright, whenever possible.
There are three main reasons for this:
• The diaphragm can move lower, giving a clearer view of the lungs.
• Air and fluid levels in the chest become easier to see.
• It helps prevent the blood vessels in the lungs from looking overly full or swollen (engorged).

PA vs. AP Chest X-rays
When chest X-rays are taken using an AP (anteroposterior) view at a 72-inch (183 cm) source-toimage receptor distance (SID), the heart appears larger than it actually is. This is because the heart
sits closer to the front (anterior) of the chest, so in the AP view it is farther from the image receptor,
causing increased magnification of the heart shadow. In contrast, the PA (posteroanterior) view
places the heart closer to the image receptor, which reduces this magnification and provides a more
accurate size. Additionally, using a longer SID like 72 inches reduces magnification overall, as the Xray beam spreads out less.

Exposure Factors
Key exposure settings for upper limb radiography include:
1. Low to medium kilovoltage — 55 to 70 for analog, 60 to 80 for digital systems
2. Short exposure time
3. Use of a small focal spot
4. Sufficient mAs to ensure proper image density (or brightness)
Grids are generally not used for upper limb imaging unless the body part being examined—such as the
shoulder—measures more than 10 cm in thickness.
A standard SID for upper limb X-rays is 40 to 44 inches (102 to 112 cm). When placing the image
receptor on the tabletop instead of in the bucky tray, the tube height needs to be raised by about 3 to 4
inches (8 to 10 cm) to maintain the correct distance—especially with floating tabletops.
