I. PHYSICS AND INSTRUMENTATION
1.1 FUNDAMENTAL CONCEPTS OF MRI
What are the principles of MRI?
MRI works by using powerful magnets to generate a strong magnetic field, which causes the protons in
the body to line up with that field. When a radiofrequency pulse is applied, it causes the protons to
become energized and move out of alignment, resisting the magnetic pull.

1. 2 ATOMIC STRUCTURE
An atom is made up of a central nucleus, which contains protons and neutrons, and electrons that
move around it. Protons carry a positive charge, neutrons have no charge, and electrons are
negatively charged.
• The atomic number is the total number of protons in the nucleus, and it determines the atom's
chemical identity.
• The mass number (or atomic weight) is the combined number of protons and neutrons in the
nucleus.
The most common atom in the human body is hydrogen, which is mainly found in water and fat
molecules.

Motion in an Atom
There are three main types of movement happening inside an atom:
• Electrons spin around their own axis
• Electrons orbit around the nucleus
• The nucleus also spins around its own axis

1.3 MR-active nuclei
Only nuclei that have an odd mass number or atomic weight are used in MRI. These are called MRactive nuclei because they respond to the magnetic field.
MR-active nuclei have a natural tendency to align their spin with an applied magnetic field. This
happens because they have angular momentum (or spin) and carry a positive electrical charge due to
the protons they contain.
• The law of electromagnetic induction explains how electric and magnetic fields are linked through
motion.
• Faraday’s law, when an electric field moves, it creates a magnetic field, and when a magnetic field
changes, it can generate an electric field in return
Faraday’s Law
The amount of current that gets created (induced) depends on four main things:
1. How strong the magnetic field is (B0)
2. How fast the magnetic field moves across the conductor
3. The angle between the conductor and the magnetic field
4. The number of loops or turns in the conductor

1.6 Precession and precessional (Larmor) frequency
The influence of the main magnetic field (B₀) causes an additional spin of the magnetic moments of
hydrogen nuclei around B₀.
This secondary spin is called precession, and the speed at which the nuclei precess around B₀ is known
as the precessional frequency or Larmor frequency.
The unit of precessional frequency is Hertz (Hz), where:
• 1 Hz = 1 cycle or rotation per second
• 1 Megahertz (MHz) = 1 million cycles per second
The precessional frequency of MR-active nuclei varies depending on the strength of the external
magnetic field.

1.7 Phase:
Phase refers to the position of magnetic moments on their precessional path at any given moment in
time.
1. Out of Phase (Incoherent): Magnetic moments of hydrogen nuclei are at different positions on their
precessional paths at a given moment in time.
2. In Phase (Coherent): Magnetic moments of hydrogen nuclei are at the same position on their
precessional paths at a given moment in time.
• At equilibrium, the magnetic moments of the spins are typically out of phase with one another.

• Resonance is a phenomenon that occurs when an object is exposed to an oscillating perturbation that
has a frequency close to its own natural frequency of oscillation.
• In MRI, resonance is achieved by transmitting a radiofrequency (RF) excitation pulse, which is
generated by a transmit coil.
Consequences of RF Excitation Pulses
1. The RF excitation pulse generates an oscillating magnetic field known as B₁.
• The B₁ field is applied at 90° to the main magnetic field (B₀).
• It is delivered over a narrow range or bandwidth of frequencies, centered around a specific central
frequency.
2. Another effect of the RF excitation pulse is that it causes the
magnetic moments of spin-up and spin-down nuclei to move
into phase with each other, resulting in coherent precession.
The radiofrequency (RF) field used in MRI is often referred to as
B₁, which is a secondary magnetic field applied in addition to B₀,
the main magnetic field.

1.9 MRI Signal and FID
How is the MRI Signal Generated?
According to Faraday’s Law, a changing magnetic field induces the movement of charged particles
(electrons), creating an electric current.
• In MRI, the magnetization vector precessing in the transverse plane creates this changing magnetic
field.
• When a receiver coil or any conductive loop is placed within this moving magnetic field, a voltage is
induced in the coil.
• This voltage is known as the

MRI signal.

What is FID (Free Induction Decay)?
• After the RF excitation pulse is turned off, the magnetic moments of the hydrogen nuclei
begin to lose phase coherence (they dephase).
• As a result, a decaying voltage is induced in the receiver coil.
• This signal is known as Free Induction Decay (FID).

What Happens When the RF Pulse is Switched Off?
1. Hydrogen nuclei begin to lose the energy received from the RF excitation pulse — a process known as
relaxation.
2. The Net Magnetization Vector (NMV) gradually realigns with B₀.
3. Simultaneously, the magnetic moments of the hydrogen nuclei lose coherence (dephase).
4. Longitudinal magnetization begins to recover — this is known as T1 recovery.
5. Transverse magnetization begins to decay — this is known as T2 decay.
6. The induced signal in the receiver coil decreases, which is observed as the FID.
Pulse Timing Parameters
TR (Repetition Time):
• Time between successive RF excitation pulses for each slice.
• Measured in milliseconds (ms).
• Determines the amount of T1 relaxation before the signal is read.
TE (Echo Time):
• Time from the RF pulse to the peak of the signal received in the coil.
• Also measured in milliseconds (ms).
• Determines the amount of T2 relaxation captured in the signal.

2. Data Acquisition

2.1 IMAGE WEIGHTING AND CONTRAST
Relaxation is the process by which magnetization changes after the RF excitation pulse is turned off.
•It leads to:
• Recovery of longitudinal magnetization — a process called T1 recovery.
• Decay of coherent transverse magnetization — a process called T2 decay.
T1 Recovery:
• Occurs when hydrogen nuclei release energy to their surrounding environment (lattice).
• T1 is the time it takes for 63% of the longitudinal magnetization to recover in the tissue.
T2 Decay:
• Happens when hydrogen nuclei interact with neighboring nuclei, exchanging energy.
• These interactions cause a loss of phase coherence and transverse magnetization.
• T2 is the time it takes for 63% of the transverse magnetization to decay.

Four Basic Steps to Generate an MRI Image
1. The patient is positioned inside the MRI magnet.
2. A radiofrequency (RF) pulse is briefly sent into the body and then turned off.
3. The body emits a signal as the nuclei relax.
4. This signal is collected and used to reconstruct the MRI image.

1. T1 Weighting
• TR (Repetition Time) determines how much T1 recovery occurs before the next RF pulse.
• TR controls T1 contrast.
• For T1-weighted images:
• TR must be short (so fat and water don’t fully realign with B₀).
• TE must also be short.
• T1-weighted images are commonly used for:
• Anatomy visualization
• Detecting pathology post-contrast administration

2.2 Spin-echo pulse sequences
What Are Pulse Sequences?
Pulse sequences are techniques used by the MRI system to rephase the magnetic moments of
hydrogen nuclei at a later time, producing a signal called an echo.
• Sequences that use a 180° RF rephasing pulse to create an echo are called spin-echo pulse
sequences.
• Sequences that use a gradient instead of a 180° pulse are called gradient-echo pulse
sequences
Conventional Spin-Echo (SE)
• Conventional spin-echo is typically used to acquire one or two echoes to achieve T1, T2, or proton
density (PD) weighting.
Turbo Spin Echo (TSE)
• TSE is a fast MRI sequence that significantly reduces scan times.
• In TSE, several 180° refocusing RF pulses are delivered during each TR (repetition time) interval.
• Between each echo, the phase-encoding gradient is briefly switched on.

Fast Spin Echo (FSE)
• In FSE, a 90° excitation RF pulse is followed by several 180° rephasing RF pulses.
• In conventional spin-echo, only one phase-encoding step is done per TR for each slice, filling one
line of k-space at a time.
• In TSE/FSE, scan time is reduced by performing multiple phase-encoding steps per TR and filling
multiple lines of k-space.
• This is possible by using multiple 180° RF pulses to create multiple spin echoes, forming what is
known as an echo train.
Inversion Recovery (IR) Sequence
Inversion recovery (IR) is a type of spin-echo sequence that uses a 180° inverting RF pulse at the
beginning to suppress signals from certain tissues.
• After the 180° pulse, a 90° excitation pulse is applied after a specific delay called the inversion time
(TI).
• Based on the TI value, there are two main types of IR sequences:
• Short Inversion Time Recovery (STIR)
• Fluid-Attenuated Inversion Recovery (FLAIR)

2.4 Gradient-echo pulse sequences
Gradient-echo pulse sequences use different flip angles and shorter repetition times (TR). Instead
of 180° RF pulses like in spin-echo sequences, gradients are applied to rephase the protons. Since
gradients can be turned on and off quickly, rephasing happens faster, which, along with shorter TR
and smaller flip angles, leads to much faster scan times.
In gradient-echo sequences, we can achieve T1 weighting, proton density weighting, and T2*
weighting.
Gradient-echo sequences are different from spin-echo sequences in two main ways:
1. They use variable RF excitation pulse flip angles, instead of always using a 90° flip angle like spinecho.
2. They rephase the protons using magnetic field gradients, not RF pulses.
The goal of these differences is to allow shorter TRs, meaning faster imaging compared to spin-echo
sequences.
Because low flip angles are used, only a small amount of longitudinal magnetization is turned into
transverse magnetization during excitation. As a result, relaxation happens faster, and a short TR can be
used.

2.5 Difference between FSE and GRE
• Fast Spin Echo (FSE):
FSE uses many 180° refocusing pulses one after another to speed up the scan.
This helps reduce scan time while keeping true T2 signal contrast.
• Gradient Echo (GRE):
GRE does not use a 180° pulse.
Instead, it relies on T2* dephasing, which happens much quicker than regular T2 decay.
GRE sequences are overall much faster and allow very quick image repetition.
Fast Gradient Echo:
• Fast gradient echo imaging uses very short TR times.
• When RF pulses are sent very quickly (TR is shorter than T2), the system reaches a steady state —
a balance between excitation and relaxation.
• This technique is especially helpful when high temporal resolution is needed, like in dynamic
imaging.

Echo Planar Imaging (EPI):
• EPI is a very fast MRI technique where multiple echoes are collected using rephasing gradients
instead of sending many 180° RF pulses (like in regular spin echo).
• This is done by quickly switching the readout or frequency-encoding gradient back and forth.
• EPI can use:
• Only gradient echoes, or
• A mix of a spin echo followed by a train of gradient echoes.
Types of EPI:
• Gradient-Echo EPI (GE-EPI):
Starts with an RF excitation pulse (any flip angle), then collects multiple gradient-echoes.
• Spin-Echo EPI (SE-EPI):
Starts with a 90° RF excitation pulse → followed by a 180° RF rephasing pulse → then collects multiple
gradient-echoes.
Uses of EPI:
• Helpful in imaging the heart and coronary vessels.
• Useful in interventional procedures.
• Enables fast imaging of physiology, like perfusion and blood oxygenation.

3.1 SPATIAL ENCODING
Mechanism of Gradients
• In MRI, we use gradients to locate where the signal is coming from.
• Without a gradient, all hydrogen nuclei spin (precess) at the same frequency because they
experience the same magnetic field.
• To find the location of the signals, we change the main magnetic field to slope linearly — making
the field stronger or weaker depending on position.
• This slope is created by gradient coils, which are cylindrical electromagnets placed inside the
warm bore of the MRI machine.
Gradient Coils:
• When current passes through the gradient coil, it causes a linear change in the magnetic field.
• The amount of current controls:
• Amplitude
• Strength
• Slope of the gradient (steep or shallow)

Magnetic Isocenter:
• The magnetic isocenter is the central point where all three gradient axes and the magnet bore
meet.
• Even when gradients are applied, at the magnetic isocenter:
• The magnetic field strength stays the same.
• The precessional frequency and phase do not change.
Polarity:
• The direction of the current through the gradient coil decides the polarity (whether the gradient
increases or decreases the field in a particular direction).

Gradient Coils in MRI:
Inside the bore of the MRI machine, there are three gradient coils, and they are named based on the

• Z-gradient:

• X-gradient:
Changes the magnetic field along the horizontal axis — from the right side to the left side of the

1. Signal-to-noise ratio (SNR)
SNR is the ratio between the strength of the received signal and the average strength of the
background noise.

• Coil Positioning
The position of the coil is crucial for maximizing SNR. To induce maximum
signal, the coil must be positioned in the transverse plane perpendicular
to B₀.
• Proton Density and SNR
Areas with low proton density (such as the lungs) generate low signal and
therefore low SNR. In contrast, areas with high proton density (such as
the pelvis) produce high signal and therefore high SNR.
• Voxel Size and SNR
Large voxels contain more spins or nuclei compared to small voxels. As a
result, they contribute more to the signal, resulting in a higher SNR. Thus,
SNR is proportional to voxel volume.
• Source of Noise in MRI
Noise in MRI primarily arises from the thermodynamic motion of
electrons in both the coil and the body tissues. This noise occurs across
all frequencies and affects the entire image unless filtered or smoothed.

2. Contrast-to-Noise Ratio (CNR)
• CNR measures the difference in SNR between two neighboring areas.
• It’s the most important factor that affects the quality of an MRI image.
• A higher CNR makes it easier to clearly see pathology apart from normal tissue, or to clearly tell two
different structures apart.
How to improve CNR:
1. Increase the signal from the pathology or important structures.
2. Decrease the signal from surrounding normal tissues.
Special Technique:
• In gradient-echo sequences, out-of-phase imaging (Dixon technique) is used to cancel out signal
from voxels that have both fat and water.
• This is done by choosing a TE where fat and water signals are out of phase, so their signals cancel each
other, resulting in no signal from those voxels.

3. Spatial Resolution
• Spatial resolution is how well we can tell two points apart as separate and clear.
• It mainly depends on the voxel size.
• Small voxels = high spatial resolution → small details are easy to see.
• Large voxels = low spatial resolution → small structures blur together.
• In big voxels, different signal intensities mix together, and we can’t see small differences
clearly. This effect is called partial voluming.
Voxel size is influenced by:
1. Slice thickness
2. Field of View (FOV)
3. Matrix size (number of pixels)

Rectangular FOV
• When the anatomy is smaller along the phase axis compared to the frequency axis, we can use a

• NSA (Number of Signal Averages)

• Low NSA

5.1 Instrumentation
All MRI machines must have the following main parts:
1. A strong magnet to create the main magnetic field.
2. A shim system to make the magnetic field more uniform.
3. A gradient system to create linear changes (slopes) in the magnetic field in different directions.
4. An RF transmission system to send out pulses of electromagnetic energy.
5. RF receiver coils to pick up the signals coming from the patient's body.
6. A computer system to enter scan settings and display the images.
7. A computer subsystem to control the timing of RF pulses and gradients, reconstruct the signals
into images, and store the images.

Magnetism
Paramagnetism
• Paramagnetic materials boost the magnetic field when placed inside it.
• Examples: calcium, oxygen, and metals like aluminum, titanium, and platinum.
Diamagnetism
• Diamagnetic materials slightly weaken the magnetic field.
• Examples: hydrogen, helium, and metals like gold, silver, and lead.
Ferromagnetism
• Ferromagnetic materials are strongly pulled into the magnetic field and line up with it.
• They can cause image distortion in MRI if patients have ferromagnetic implants.
• Only four elements are naturally ferromagnetic at room temperature: iron, nickel, cobalt.

Basically, there are two main types of receiver coils.
1. Surface coils
are used for imaging surface anatomy.
They are considered receive-only coils and are often set up as a phased array.
Surface coils are placed flat and close to the area being scanned, providing the best filling factor.
Another reason they deliver a high SNR (Signal-to-Noise Ratio) is because they only pick up noise from a
small area.
However, they are not suitable for imaging structures deep inside the body.
An example is the linear phased array used for spine imaging.
2. Volume coils
are used when scanning a large volume of tissue.
Their main advantage is that they provide a more even RF field across a big area, unlike surface coils.
The downside is that they usually have a lower filling factor compared to surface coils — meaning the coil
has empty spaces not filled by anatomy, which reduces the SNR.
Also, parts of the anatomy inside the coil that aren’t being imaged can still add thermal noise without
giving useful signal.

III. Imaging Procedure and protocols
Tissue Suppression and Brain Imaging
• Tissue suppression is mainly applied to differentiate fat from enhancing pathology, especially in
T1-weighted images and FSE T2-weighted images where fat and abnormal tissue can look
similar.

Brain Imaging
• T2-FLAIR: Helpful when performed after giving contrast. Because FLAIR uses inversion recovery (IR)
properties, lesions that enhance after contrast appear bright, which makes detecting leptomeningeal
metastases or meningitis easier.
• In patients who are uncooperative, fast sequences like SS-FSE or SS-EPI are used to complete scans
quickly.
• IR T1 Sequences: Particularly important for imaging young children. Since white matter doesn’t fully
myelinate until about 5 years of age, grey and white matter can look very similar on regular SE T1
scans. IR T1 improves the contrast between them.
• FLAIR Imaging: In these images, periventricular abnormalities stand out with a high signal while the
CSF appears dark because it has been suppressed using a long inversion time (TI).
• GRE Sequences: More sensitive to magnetic susceptibility effects, making them better than SE or
FSE for detecting hemorrhages.

Temporal Lobe Imaging
• Temporal lobe lesions are often very small, so using volume acquisitions helps by providing very

• IR FSE T2 sequences are particularly good because they create strong contrast between grey and

Pituitary Fossa Imaging
• For the pituitary gland, coronal sections are best.
• Contrast isn’t always needed, but it becomes important for cases like diabetes insipidus or

Abdomen MRI
Liver Imaging

• The liver is the most frequently scanned organ in abdominal MRI.
• After administering contrast, delayed imaging combined with fat suppression is often necessary to
assess the arterial, venous, and equilibrium phases.
• SS-FSE MRCP: This technique generates images showing only fluid-filled structures like the
gallbladder and bile ducts. It requires very long echo times (TEs over 200 ms) and repetition times
(TRs longer than 10 seconds) to suppress signals from all tissues except those with long T2 decay
times.
• Diffusion Tensor Imaging (DTI) combined with parallel imaging can help differentiate benign from
malignant liver lesions and measure liver fibrosis. Using multi-array coils for parallel imaging
significantly cuts down scan time.
• Haemangioma Evaluation: Two FSE sequences with echo times of 80 ms and 160 ms are used
because haemangiomas maintain high signal intensity on longer echoes.
• T1-weighted Post-Contrast Imaging: Acquired with tissue suppression and in multiple phases, this
technique helps in analyzing how hepatic lesions enhance over time.

Cervical Spine
A sagittal STIR sequence is helpful for trauma cases, especially to show muscle injuries. STIR is

Breast DWI and Dynamic Imaging
Diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) of the breast may help distinguish between benign and malignant
lesions and could be useful for monitoring how metastatic disease responds to chemotherapy.
Dynamic imaging assesses the blood flow (haemodynamics) of a breast lesion:
• Lesions that enhance (wash in and wash out) quickly are more likely to be malignant.
• Lesions that enhance and drain slowly are more likely to be benign
Timing for Breast MRI
The best time to scan for breast cancer is about 10 days after menstruation ends, as hormonal
changes during other parts of the cycle can cause normal breast tissue to enhance, possibly hiding
small tumors.
Breast MRI Sequences
The standard approach includes:
• A T2-weighted bright-fluid sequence.
• Multiphase T1-weighted fat-suppressed sequences.
Additional sequences and post-processing techniques can be added as needed. When reporting,
the BI-RADS system should be used.

Sagittal Plane
• Used to spot injuries to the cruciate ligaments, menisci, popliteal cysts, and patellar tendon.
• High-resolution images are needed (pixel size <0.45 mm).
• For ligament visualization, sagittal proton density (PD)-weighted sequences without fat
suppression are ideal, where ligaments appear dark grey against bright fat.
• To enhance lesion detection in ligaments, use echo times (TEs) between 45–65 ms to increase
contrast-to-noise ratio (CNR).

Coronal Plane
• Best for detecting meniscal tears.
• Requires high spatial resolution (pixel size <0.45 mm) and high sensitivity to fluid.

Shoulder MRI
The axial view is key for evaluating joint cartilage, the glenoid labrum, bone changes like Hill–Sachs
deformity, and the muscles and tendons of the rotator cuff.

Fat-Suppressed T2 Imaging
• Fat-suppressed T2-weighted sequences are excellent for spotting muscle tears, trabecular
bone injury, joint fluid, and tendon tears.
• If spin echo (SE) sequences are used, fat suppression might not be needed.
• On most MRI systems, the strength of fat suppression can be adjusted—reducing it helps improve
the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR).
Cartilage Lesions
• Cartilage damage is easier to detect with longer echo times (TE of at least 30–40 ms), as this
reduces the signal from healthy cartilage and highlights abnormalities.
Preferred Sequences
• Spin Echo (SE) and Fast Spin Echo (FSE) are typically the main sequences used.
• Coherent Gradient Echo (GRE) and Short Tau Inversion Recovery (STIR) sequences are also helpful,
especially for visualizing joint fluid.
• If magnet shimming is not ideal, STIR might give better results compared to fat-suppressed FSE.

Contrast agents in MRI
Gadolinium-based Contrast Material (GBCM) Calculation
First, you need to know the standard dose:
• 0.1 mmol/kg
• 0.2 mL/kg
To calculate how much the patient should receive, you need two things:
• Weight (kg)
• Type of GBCM (concentration)
More clarification (There are 2 main types):
• Omniscan or Dotarem: 1 CC for every 10 kg
The concentration of these types is: 0.5 mmol/mL
To calculate the dose: (Weight × 0.2) or (Weight ÷ 10 ÷ 2)
Example: Weight = 80 kg ➝ Dose = 16 mL - Weight = 120 kg ➝ Dose = 24 mL
• Gadavist or Primovist : 1 CC for every 10 kg
The concentration of these types is: 1.0 mmol/mL or 0.25 mmol/mL
To calculate the dose: (Weight × 0.1) or (Weight ÷ 10)
Example: Weight = 70 kg ➝ Dose = 7 mL - Weight = 110 kg ➝ Dose = 11 mL

Types of MRI Coils
Volume Coils
1. Provide better RF homogeneity than surface coils.

Surface Coils
1. Used to improve the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) and signal intensity.
Example: Rectal coil
Phased Array Coils
1. Combine the advantages of surface coils (improved SNR) and volume coils (increased coverage).
