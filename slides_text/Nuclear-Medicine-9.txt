1. PHYSICS AND INSTRUMENTATION
Nuclear Force and Binding Energy
• The nuclear force is a powerful binding force that holds nucleons together tightly.
• The work (energy) required to overcome this force and remove a nucleon from the nucleus is called the
nuclear binding energy.

Radioactive Materials
• An element with an unstable nucleus (excess energy).
• An unstable nucleus will adjust itself to a more stable state by:
• Ejecting portions of its nucleus (alpha or beta particles).
• Emitting energy in the form of photons (gamma rays).
• This process is called radioactive decay.

A = number of protons + number of neutrons
Z = number of protons
A - Z = number of neutrons
Number of neutrons = Mass Number - Atomic Number

Half-Life
• The time it takes for half of the atoms to decay is called the physical half-life.
Radioactive Materials
• Uranium
• Thorium
• Radium
• Polonium
Desirable Characteristics for Radiopharmaceuticals
1. Physical half-life of a few hours, equal to the duration of preparation and injection.
2. Should emit gamma rays (50–300 keV); decay by isomeric transition or electron capture is preferable.
3. Easily attaches to a pharmaceutical at room temperature.
4. Easily eliminated from the body, with an effective half-life similar to the duration of the examination.

99Mo–99mTc Generator
• A radionuclide “parent” with a long half-life is bound to an ion exchange alumina column.
• Parent isotope: ⁹⁹Mo (67-hour half-life).
• Daughter isotope: ⁹⁹ᵐTc (6-hour half-life).
• ⁹⁹ᵐTc is loosely bound and removed (“eluted”) with an elution liquid such as 0.9% saline.
Wet vs. Dry Generator Systems
1. Wet system: Contains a saline reservoir and a vacuum vial to draw saline across the column.
2. Dry system: Requires placing a vial with a specific amount of saline on the entry port, drawn across by
a vacuum vial.
Technetium-99m Valence State
• Eluted ⁹⁹ᵐTc is in the +7 valence state.
• Tin is used as a reducing agent to allow labeling of other compounds.
Breakthrough
• Molybdenum breakthrough: Detected by 740–780 keV photons penetrating a lead shield that blocks 140 keV
photons of ⁹⁹ᵐTc.
• Aluminum ion breakthrough: Detected with special test paper that changes color. Excess aluminum indicates
instability of the generator column and appears as high uptake in the liver.

GEIGER-MUELLER COUNTER:
• Handheld, very sensitive, inexpensive survey instruments.
• Primarily used to detect small amounts of radioactive contamination.
• Gas-filled detector.

2. SCINTILLATION CAMERA
GAMMA CAMERA:
• The most widely used cameras in nuclear medicine are:
• Simple gamma scintillation (Anger) camera

FUNCTION OF GAMMA CAMERA:
• Converts photons emitted by the radionuclide in the patient into a light pulse, and subsequently into a

CRYSTAL
• The most common crystal used in gamma cameras is Sodium Iodide (NaI).
• Typical thickness: 1 cm.
• Trade-off:
• Thicker crystals → absorb a larger proportion of gamma rays but allow more scatter → poorer spatial
resolution.
• Thinner crystals → better resolution but less absorption.
RESOLUTION
• Refers to the ability of a gamma camera to distinguish between adjacent points in an organ.
SENSITIVITY
• Refers to the ability of an imaging camera to detect the photons generated by the nuclides used.
• Low sensitivity system: detects fewer photons.
• High sensitivity system: detects more photons.

Sensitivity in Nuclear Medicine:
• Sensitivity (or efficiency) = fraction of incident photons that an imaging system actually records.
• High-energy photons → more penetrating → less likely to interact.
• Gamma cameras perform best with photons in the 100–200 keV energy range.
Trade-off:
• High sensitivity → relatively low resolution.
• High resolution → relatively low sensitivity.
RESOLUTION & COLLIMATOR SEPTA
• A. Longer septa
• Attenuate most photons except those exactly perpendicular to
the crystal face.
• Increases selectivity → increases resolution.
• However, decreases the count rate detected.
• B. Shorter septa
• Allow more photons to reach the crystal → higher count rate.
• But spatial resolution is decreased, because photons entering
through each hole originate from a larger area.

EFFECT OF SOURCE-TO-CAMERA DISTANCE
• A. Source far from camera head
• Many photons reach the crystal in an almost perpendicular

• Produces a large area of impact on the crystal.
• Increases uncertainty about the exact location of the source.
B. Source close to camera head
• Scintillation events in the crystal correspond more accurately to
the actual source location.
• Resolution is improved.

PET/CT SYSTEM
• Provides efficient and accurate attenuation correction.
• Shorter imaging times.
• More precise anatomic localization of lesions.
• Allows acquisition of diagnostic CT and PET scans in a single session.
PET/MRI SYSTEM
• Useful for brain pathology (stroke, tumors) and whole-body oncologic applications (e.g., liver metastases).

• Lower radiation dose compared with PET/CT.

DIFFERENCE BETWEEN GAMMA CAMERA AND SPECT
• Gamma Camera:
• Detects gamma photons using crystals in the camera.
• Produces a 2D projection of a 3D object (similar to an X-ray).
• SPECT:
• A tomographic version of the gamma camera.
• Produces cross-sectional slices through the body.
WHY IS SPECT/CT BETTER THAN SPECT?
• Fusion of anatomical (CT) and functional (SPECT) information.
• Improves image interpretation.
• Adds diagnostic value compared with SPECT alone.
IS SPECT MORE SENSITIVE THAN PET?
• No. PET has:
• Higher spatial resolution.
• Higher sensitivity for signals.
• Higher image quality.

WHICH IS BETTER: SPECT OR PET?
• PET has superior image quality and higher resolution.
• PET images generally have fewer artifacts.
• PET can provide quantitative estimates of blood flow.
DIFFERENCE BETWEEN CT, MRI, PET, AND SPECT
• CT & MRI: show form and structure of tissues/organs.
• PET & SPECT: show function and physiology, highlighting unusual cellular activity.
• PET: used in cancer, heart disease, brain disorders.
SOURCES OF ARTIFACTS IN NUCLEAR MEDICINE (can be manipulated by 3 ways):
1. Attenuation Correction – reduces artifacts from photons attenuated by overlying soft tissue.
2. Motion Correction – compensates for artifacts caused by patient movement (vertical, horizontal, or
rotational).
3. Scatter Correction – reduces artifacts from photons scattered during imaging.

2. Patient Care
Pregnancy and Radio-Pharmaceuticals
• The use of radio-pharmaceuticals is discouraged during pregnancy.
• Iodine crosses the placenta, and radioiodine therapy can damage the fetal thyroid.
• Women are advised to avoid pregnancy for at least one year after radioiodine treatment.
• The IAEA recommends using the lowest possible dose, along with hydration and frequent voiding, to
minimize fetal radiation exposure.
• Hydration without voiding actually increases fetal dose.
• The maximum allowed fetal radiation dose from maternal occupational exposure is 0.5 rem.
Preparations for Radio-Iodine Therapy
• All patients scheduled for nuclear medicine procedures should be screened for pregnancy and
breastfeeding.
• Fasting improves radioiodine absorption.
• Patients should avoid iodine-containing substances (contrast media, foods, thyroid medications, or drugs
interfering with iodine uptake).

Bone Scan
• Radio-pharmaceuticals used for bone scans are excreted via urine, giving the bladder the highest radiation
dose.
• Hydration and frequent voiding help lower radiation exposure to the bladder.
DaT Scan
• DaTscan® is injected intravenously to evaluate dopamine-containing neurons, which control movement.
• It has high binding affinity to presynaptic dopamine transporters, which are reduced in Parkinsonian
syndromes.
Thyroid Uptake Probe
• A pinhole collimator may be used for thyroid imaging.
• For uptake measurements, a flat field collimator is preferred.
Common Sites of Bone Metastasis
• Prostate, lung, and breast cancers commonly spread to bone.
• The most frequent metastatic sites are the thoracic bones, spine, and pelvis (axial skeleton).

Radiation Safety & Special Considerations
Radiopharmaceutical Spills
• The first priority in any spill is to contain the contamination and prevent it from spreading.
• If 99mTc spills on clothing, the contaminated clothing should be removed and stored until the radioactivity
decays to background levels.
• For floor spills, access to the contaminated area should be restricted immediately.
• Chelating agents may alter the chemical form of the material but do not reduce its radioactivity.
• The Radiation Safety Officer (RSO) must be notified after initial containment.
Diphosphonate Kits
• Although package inserts state that radiopharmaceuticals prepared from diphosphonate kits are usable for
6–8 hours, most imaging centers prefer to use them within 4 hours for optimal image quality.
Breastfeeding Considerations in Nuclear Medicine
• 18F-FDG: breastfeeding does not need to be interrupted.
• Technetium-based radiopharmaceuticals: interruption ranges from 0 to 24 hours, depending on the agent.
• Thallium-201, Gallium-67, Indium-111: breastfeeding is usually discontinued for at least a week.
• Radioiodine therapy: breastfeeding is contraindicated for several months after administration.

Measuring Personnel Radiation Exposure
• Geiger-Müller counters are not used for personal exposure monitoring because of dead time limitations—
they are better suited for detecting small amounts of radiation.
• Personnel exposure is instead monitored using dosimeters (e.g., film badges, TLDs, OSL dosimeters).
Radiation Dose to the Embryo/Fetus
• A declared pregnant worker must ensure fetal radiation exposure remains below 0.5 rem (5 mSv) for the
entire pregnancy from occupational sources.

Whole Body Bone Scan
Indication
• Detection of bone metastases for staging in patients with known or suspected cancers (breast, lung,
prostate, kidney).
• Evaluation of response to therapy (chemotherapy or radiotherapy).
Patient Preparation
• Drink plenty of fluids before and after dose administration.
• Return 2–4 hours post-injection for imaging
Equipment
• Camera: Large field of view.
• Collimator: Low Energy High Resolution (LEHR) or Low Energy All Purpose (LEAP).
Procedure:
• Withdraw 20–25 mCi of 99mTc-MDP or HDP in a 3–5 mL syringe.
• Administer dose intravenously, followed by at least 10 mL normal saline.
• Advise patient to drink plenty of fluids and empty bladder to clear soft tissue activity.
• If a lesion is detected → take static or SPECT/CT images for localization.

Thyroid Uptake and Scan (Tc-99m)
Indications
• Assessment of thyroid function in cases of suspected hyperthyroidism or hypothyroidism (abnormal T3 and T4
levels).
• Evaluation of abnormal thyroid hormone results, particularly TSH.
• Assessment of thyroid structure, including gland position, goiter, surgical changes, hot and cold nodules, or

Patient Preparation
• Patients must stop thyroid-related medications (such as thyroxine or antithyroid drugs) before the procedure.
Equipment
• Gamma camera: large field of view.
• Collimator: Low Energy High Resolution (LEHR), Low Energy All Purpose (LEAP), or pinhole collimator.
Procedure
• Prepare 5 mCi of technetium pertechnetate (TcO₄) in a 3–5 mL syringe.
• Take a pre-injection syringe count using the gamma camera (1 minute).
To follow any updates/courses:
Group: t.me/SRTLE1

Nuclear Medicine for Radiologic Technologist

Procedure
• Administer the tracer intravenously, followed by at least 10 mL of saline. Instruct the patient to drink water
to wash tracer activity from the esophagus.
• Record the residual syringe count after injection.
• After 15–20 minutes, position the patient supine with arms at the side and neck hyperextended (chin up).
• Use a marker to detect possible ectopic thyroid tissue.
Normal Findings
• Euthyroid: Homogeneous tracer distribution with uptake between 0.5–4%.
• The left lobe is smaller than the right, and a pyramidal lobe may be present.
• The thyroid isthmus is usually not visualized.
• Outer margins appear straight or slightly convex.
• Thyroid uptake is equal to or slightly greater than salivary gland uptake.
• Tracer activity is also seen in salivary glands, soft tissues, brain, stomach lining, nasopharynx, and bladder
Abnormal Findings
• Hyperthyroidism: Uptake exceeds 4%.
• Hypothyroidism: Uptake is less than 0.5%.
Additional Notes
• Technetium pertechnetate (TcO₄⁻) is trapped by thyroid cells but not organified, while radioactive iodine is
both trapped and organified to produce thyroid hormones (T3 and T4).

Normal Findings
Early images: heterogeneous tracer uptake in the thyroid, salivary glands, heart, and gastrointestinal tract.
Delayed images: washout of tracer without persistent focal uptake.
Abnormal Findings
• Delayed images: thyroid activity washes out but focal persistent uptake is seen (commonly from salivary

Additional Notes
• Around 80% of people have four parathyroid glands; 15% may have more, while 5% may have fewer.
• Normal glands are only a few millimeters in size, located at the upper and lower poles of the thyroid gland.
• Parathyroid hormone (PTH) regulates calcium and phosphorus levels in the blood.

Procedure
• Administer 8–12 mCi of 99mTc-DTPA or 5 mCi of 99mTc-MAG3 intravenously using a 3–5 mL syringe.
• Position detector close to the kidneys for better resolution.
• Instruct the patient to remain still throughout, as dynamic images cannot be repeated.
• If needed, obtain delayed images at 30–60 minutes.
Normal Findings
• On flow images, both kidneys appear simultaneously, with similar size and intensity.
• Time to peak (Tmax): 3–6 minutes.
• Clearance (T½): 12–15 minutes for DTPA, 8–12 minutes for MAG3.
Abnormal Findings
• On flow acquisition, one or both kidneys may fail to appear or appear at different times.
• Pyelonephritis: enlarged kidney with reduced activity.
• Renal artery stenosis or hydronephrosis: asymmetric uptake.
• Renal failure: increased uptake in liver and spleen.
Additional Notes
• MAG3 cannot be used to measure GFR, as it binds strongly to plasma proteins.
• DTPA is ideal for GFR measurement: ~95% remains unbound and is filtered through the glomeruli.

Cardiac stress test
Indications:
• Detection and evaluation of coronary artery disease (CAD).
• Uptake of sestamibi, tetrofosmin, and thallium in myocardium is proportional to blood flow.
• Under stress → increased flow requirements.
• Diseased vessels → diminished/restricted flow → defects visible.
• Results usually compared with rest test (sometimes stress-only if normal).
• Evaluation of suspected/known coronary artery stenosis.
• Clinical indicators: acute myocardial infarction, chest pain, shortness of breath, history/family history of
heart disease.
Patient Preparation:
• Radiopharmaceuticals:
• Tc-99m Sestamibi or Tetrofosmin: 20–30 mCi
• Thallium-201: 3–5 mCi
• NPO 4–12 hours (diabetics: 2 hours).
• Clear liquids/crackers allowed for sestamibi/tetrofosmin.
• Nothing at all for thallium (except orange juice allowed).
• Diabetics: monitor blood sugar, adjust medications as needed.

Lung perfusion
Indications
• Evaluation for pulmonary embolism (PE), the main cause being deep vein thromboses (DVTs), clots from
recent surgery or trauma.
• If perfusion defects match the same areas as ventilation defects → Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease
(COPD) is the likely diagnosis.
• If perfusion defects mismatch ventilation defects → PE is the likely diagnosis.
• A reverse mismatch is most commonly due to atelectasis (collapsed lung).
Preparation
• Obtain a recent chest X-ray (within 24 hours), or suggest ordering one.
• If the patient is symptomatic, obtain a more recent chest X-ray (within 1 hour).
• Collect any recent reports and images from related procedures.
• Obtain a complete history and a list of all medications.
• To assist distribution to lungs, instruct the patient to cough and take several deep breaths before injection.
• Ensure the dose in the syringe is thoroughly mixed before injection.

Flow Study

Static Study
• Administer injection to patient in supine position.

• Typical views: Anterior, RAO, RLat, RPO, Posterior, LPO, LLat, LAO (or as per protocol).
• A lung transmission (nuclear X-ray) image may also be taken, but it should precede ventilation or

• Acquire anterior, posterior, and right & left lateral views of the head to verify shunt.
• Acquire anterior/posterior whole-body sweep with a dual-head camera (preferred) if quantitation is
desired.

2. Cyclotrons
1. Circular devices where charged particles (e.g., protons, alpha particles) are accelerated in a spiral
path under vacuum.
2. Used to produce radionuclides for imaging and therapy.
3. Reactors
1. Composed of fuel rods containing large atoms.
2. During fission, 2–3 neutrons and approximately 200 MeV of heat energy are released.
3. Neutrons produced are used to generate radionuclides.
Emission Isotopes
• Tc-99m
• Pure gamma emitter
• Used mainly for diagnostic purposes (imaging)
• Gamma emission useful for diagnostic purposes (140 keV)
• I-131
• Emits both gamma and beta radiation
• In the past, it was used for both diagnosis and therapy
• Now, it is used only for therapy
• Beta emission is useful for therapeutic planning (364 keV)
