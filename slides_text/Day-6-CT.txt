1.1 FUNDAMENTAL CONCEPTS OF CT
In CT imaging, three axes define the scan: the X-axis (left to right), the Y-axis (front to back), and the
Z-axis (head to foot). The Z-axis determines slice thickness, while the X and Y axes define the in-plane
resolution and field of view. Together, they control image detail and diagnostic quality.

The Z axis defines the slice thickness.

1.2 Generations of CT scanners:
First Generation:
• Consists of a single X-ray tube and a single detector.
• Operates using the translate-rotate technique, where the tube and detector move linearly across the body,
then rotate slightly and repeat the process scanning take a long time, so this design is no longer used.
Second Generation:
• It contains a row of detectors.
• It uses the same principle of transition-rotation but at a higher speed due to the increased number of
detectors. Although scan times were shorter, they were still quite long, and this design is no longer in use.
Third Generation:
• It is based on the rotate-rotate technique, with a complete row of detectors rotate together around the
patient. Most commonly used CT type
Fourth Generation:
• It contains a full ring of detectors fixed around the patient, while only the x-ray tube rotates. It is
characterized by the lack of movement of the detectors, which reduces certain types of image distortions,
but it is more expensive.

1.3 Beam Attenuation
• CT Image Shades: Different structures appear as varying shades of gray, created based on radiation
principles.
• X-ray Beam: Consists of energy called photons. The reduction of the x-ray beam by an object is known as
attenuation, which depends on the object's thickness, density, and atomic number.
• Linear Attenuation Coefficient (μ): Represents the amount of x-ray beam absorbed or scattered per unit
thickness. μ decreases with higher photon energy and increases with higher atomic number and density.
• Density Difference: To differentiate objects on a CT image, a density difference is needed.
• Contrast agents: (oral or intravenous) create a temporary artificial density difference between structures.

1.3 Hounsfield Units
In CT, we can more accurately measure the beam attenuation properties of
an object. These measurements are represented in Hounsfield units (HU),
also known as CT numbers or density values.

1.4 Polychromatic X-ray Beams
CT X-ray beams are polychromatic, meaning they contain a spectrum of photon
energies. As the beam passes through a patient, low-energy photons are absorbed
more quickly than high-energy photons.
• This process, called beam hardening, increases the average energy of the beam.
Beam hardening can cause image artifacts, especially with Filtered Back Projection
(FBP) reconstruction.
Common beam-hardening artifacts include:
• Cupping: central parts of the image appear less dense than the edges.
• Streaking: dark streaks between dense objects.

1.5 Volume Averaging
Thicker CT slices can obscure small details due to volume averaging, where different tissue densities blend into
a single value. If a suspicious mass appears unclear, using thinner slices improves image accuracy and detail.
The process in CT by which different tissue attenuation values are
averaged to produce one less accurate pixel reading is called volume
averaging

1.6 Imaging Planes
CT images can be displayed in different anatomical planes to provide a complete
view of the patient’s anatomy:
•Axial (Transverse) – horizontal slices from head to foot.
•Coronal – vertical slices from front to back.
•Sagittal – vertical slices from left to right.

1.7 Pitch
• a pitch between 1 and 1.5 is common in both SDCT and MDCT systems.
• Generally, increasing the pitch leads to a reduction in image resolution.

Q

Pitch is a parameter that is commonly used to describe the CT table
movement throughout a helical scan acquisition. It is most commonly
defined as the travel distance of the CT scan table per 360 rotation of
the x-ray tube, divided by the x-ray beam collimation width

Increasing the pitch results in:
1. A reduction in scan time.
2. Less concentration of radiation.
3. A decrease in exposure dose.
4. A loss of image detail (quality decreases), leading to reduced spatial resolution.
5. The typical gantry size range is between 70 and 90 cm
6. The tilt angle usually varies from ±15° to ±30°, depending on the system.

2. DATA ACQUISITION
2.1 CT Components
Gantry
• Is the ring-shaped structure of the scanner that houses key components for producing and detecting x-rays.
• The gantry varies in size, with an aperture typically ranging from 70 to 90 cm. It can tilt ±15° to ±30° to
accommodate different patient positions and scanning protocols.

Slip rings
• This design allows continuous gantry rotation, preventing cable twisting and enabling helical scanning for
faster and more efficient imaging.

Generator
• CT scanners now use high-frequency generators
• CT generators operate at high kV to enhance beam intensity, improving x-ray penetration while lowering
patient dose. A higher kV setting also reduces the heat load on the x-ray tube by enabling the use of a
lower mA.

• These are essential to prevent overheating and ensure optimal performance of imaging components.

• CT x-ray tubes, adapted from rotating anode designs, use tungsten targets for high-intensity x-ray

2.2 Detectors
In CT systems are sensors that capture and measure the attenuated x-ray beam after it passes through the
patient, converting the transmitted radiation into electrical signals to create an image.
The design, placement, and size of CT detectors influence scatter radiation reduction, spatial resolution,
and data capture efficiency, with narrower detectors and minimal spacing improving image quality.

The key features of an ideal detector are:
• High Detector Efficiency: The ability to capture transmitted photons and convert them into electronic
signals.
• Low or No Afterglow: Minimal or no persistent flash of scintillation that could affect image
reconstruction.
• High Scatter Suppression: Effective reduction of scatter for clearer imaging.
• High Stability: Reliable performance without frequent calibration interruptions.

of most computer programming.
The Fourier Transform is a mathematical technique used to transform a function or signal from its original
domain (often time or space) into the frequency domain.
Interpolation is a mathematical method used to estimate the value of an unknown function based on the
known values on either side of it.
DATA TYPES
Raw data consists of all the measurements gathered from the detector array, and it requires significantly
more computer storage than image data.
Image data are the processed version of the raw data, where each pixel is assigned a Hounsfield unit
value.

4. IMAGE DISPLAY
4.1 Windowing in CT Imaging
The window refers to the range of CT numbers displayed in different shades of gray, from black to white.
Windowing is a post-processing technique used in CT imaging to adjust the contrast and brightness
of an image. It helps radiologic technologists and radiologists focus on specific tissues or structures
by modifying the range of Hounsfield Units (HU) displayed on the image.
1. The window level (WL) determines the center of the grayscale range displayed in the image.
The window level and brightness are concepts commonly used in CT to adjust and interpret grayscale
images effectively.

reduces contrast but allows visualization of a broader range of tissues.

What is image display in CT ?
CT images are displayed on a black-and-white or color monitor, typically using a cathode-ray tube (CRT)
or a flat-panel display like a TFT LCD (thin-film transistor, liquid crystal display).
CT scans usually start with one or more localizer images.

These images are also known as scout, surview, topogram, scanogram, preview, or pilot scans.

9.2 Patient preparation
Before starting a CT scan, the technologist must check the doctor's order and review the patient's
clinical information.
The selection of protocol should choose a protocol that best meets the patient's diagnostic needs
while reducing potential risks, such as radiation exposure, adverse reactions to contrast media, or
complications like bleeding or infections from invasive procedures.

9.3 The laboratory values
Before a routine CT scan, blood urea nitrogen (BUN) and serum creatinine levels are commonly checked.
These values help evaluate kidney function, which is crucial when administering IV contrast.
Similarly, lab values for blood coagulation, such as PT and PTT, are checked before procedures like
biopsies or fluid drainage to minimize the risk of excessive bleeding.

10. CONTRAST AGENT
Contrast administration methods vary, but in CT imaging, enhancement is mainly classified into two types:
• Intravascular (injected into veins or arteries).
• Gastrointestinal (administered orally or rectally for digestive tract visualization).
Contrast agents fill structures with substances that have different densities than the surrounding tissue.
Positive Agents: These have a higher density than the structure (e.g., barium and iodine-based agents).
Negative Agents: These have a lower density, such as air or carbon dioxide.
Neutral Agents: These have a density similar to water, often used in gastrointestinal imaging.

10.1 INTRAVASCULAR CONTRAST AGENTS
Iodinated contrast agents are widely used in radiology due to
• Water solubility
• Easy intravenous administration
• High safety profile
• Other substances tested for intravascular use were not commercially adopted due to harmful toxic effects

Properties of Iodinated Agents
Osmolality
Osmolality refers to the number of particles in a solution per unit of liquid compared to blood.
• High-osmolality contrast media can have up to seven times the osmolality of blood.
• Low-osmolality contrast media has about twice the osmolality of blood.
• Isosmolar contrast media has the same osmolality as blood.

Viscosity refers to the thickness or resistance to flow of intravascular contrast media. It is influenced

Contrast media reaction:
Most adverse reactions occur within minutes of injection, although some delayed reactions have been
observed. It remains unclear whether these delayed reactions are due to chemotoxic or idiosyncratic causes.
Chemotoxic Reactions
Chemotoxic reactions, caused by contrast agents' pharmacologic properties, are poorly understood and
result from factors like protein binding, enzyme inhibition, and metabolic disruption. Contrast mediainduced nephropathy is an example of a chemotoxic reaction that affects the kidneys.
Idiosyncratic reactions
Idiosyncratic reactions include all other unpredictable reactions.
They usually occur within 1 hour of contrast administration and are not dose-dependent.
The exact mechanisms behind these reactions are not well understood.
The mechanisms of idiosyncratic reactions are not well understood, and they are rarely caused by true

Computed Tomography Angiography (CTA)
• CTA is the preferred imaging technique for stable patients suspected of having pulmonary embolism (PE).
• Pulmonary angiography has traditionally been regarded as the gold standard for diagnosing acute PE.
• CTA scans for PE are performed in a caudal-to-cranial direction to minimize respiratory motion artifacts.
• The quality of the CTA exam is heavily dependent on the dose, rate, and timing of the intravenous contrast
administration, and the technologist's skill.
• To eliminate beam-hardening artifacts caused by dense contrast media in the superior vena cava, a saline
flush after the iodinated contrast injection is recommended for pulmonary CTA studies.
• In cardiac CTA, the use of beta-blockers can help temporarily lower the patient’s heart rate, reducing motion
artifacts.
The volume of contrast used in CTA primarily depends on two factors:
• Patient’s Physique (major effect): Larger individuals usually require more contrast to achieve optimal
imaging
• Scan Time (minor effect): The duration of the scan can slightly impact the contrast volume needed for
adequate enhancement.

Coronary CTA: Contrast Injection Guidelines
1. Consistently reliable contrast injection protocols are essential for accurate imaging.
2. The ideal coronary artery attenuation should fall between 350–400 Hounsfield Units (HU).
3. An optimal contrast dose ranges from 245 to 370 mg of iodine per kilogram of body weight.
4. The contrast should be injected over a period of 10 to 20 seconds.
5. A flow rate of 4 to 5 mL per second is generally preferred.
6. A saline flush must follow the contrast injection and should be administered at the same rate as the contrast.
7. Bolus tracking or a test bolus technique should be used to ensure proper timing of image acquisition.
The most reliable indicator of an acute embolus is a low-density filling defect within the vessel lumen.

CT in Musculoskeletal Imaging:
CT is essential for evaluating musculoskeletal anatomy and disease, providing detailed imaging of bones, soft
tissues, and tumors.
Advantages of CT: Offers cross-sectional views, allows comparison of both sides of the body, displays bone
and soft tissue together, provides high contrast sensitivity, and enables multiplanar and 3D reformations.
Scanning Techniques: Patient positioning is adjusted based on the body part, ensuring symmetry. Lower
extremities are scanned feet-first, while upper extremities are scanned head-first. AP and lateral scout images
help localize areas of interest.
Contrast Use: IV contrast is not routinely used in trauma cases but is helpful for detecting infections, softtissue tumors, and vascular abnormalities.
Reconstruction & Viewing: The algorithm is chosen based on clinical needs—standard for soft tissue, highresolution for bones. Images are viewed in both soft-tissue and bone window settings.
CT arthrography is useful for evaluation of the joint capsule and intracapsular structures and for finding loose
bodies within the joint.
