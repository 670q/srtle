1. RADIATION QUANTITIES AND THEIR UNITS OF MEASURE
1-Exposure
• Defined as the amount of ionizing radiation that may strike an object such as the human body.
• Radiation ionization in air is termed exposure.
• Measured by:
• Coulomb per kilogram (C/kg): used for exposure in air only.
• Roentgen (R).
• Air Kerma: another SI quantity that expresses radiation concentration transferred to a point (e.g., surface
of patient’s or radiographer’s body).
• Dose Area Product (DAP): the total of air kerma over the exposed area of the patient’s surface.
2-Absorbed Dose
• Deposition of energy per unit mass in patient tissue from ionizing radiation exposure.
• Responsible for biological damage in tissues.
• SI Unit: Gray (Gy).
• Gya: absorbed dose in air.
• Gyt: absorbed dose in tissue.

3-Equivalent Dose (EqD)
• Product of the absorbed dose in a tissue/organ and the radiation weighting factor (WR) for the type and
energy of radiation.
• Accounts for differences in the biological effect of different types of radiation.
• SI Unit: Sievert (Sv).
4-Effective Dose (EID / EfD)
• Incorporates:
• Type of radiation (x-rays, gamma, neutrons, etc.).
• Radiosensitivity of the organ or body part irradiated (using tissue weighting factors, WT).
• Provides an estimate of overall risk from exposure.
• SI Unit: Sievert (Sv).
Radiation Unit Conversions
• 1 Sv = 100 rem = 1000 mSv
(Used to measure radiation dose for workers)
• 1 Gy = 100 rad = 1000 mGy
(Usually used with patients and referred to as absorbed dose)

Stochastic Effects incidence of the radiation response or frequency of
an injury increases with increasing radiation dose and is not observed

Stochastic Effects
• Non-threshold dose–response relationship
• Linear dose–response relationship
• Incidence of response increases with increasing radiation dose
Linear means that an observed response is directly proportional to the dose.

for the non-linear.
Radiation-induced cancer, leukemia, and genetic effects follow a linear,
nonthreshold dose response relationship.

Direct and Indirect Effects
• Direct Effect:
• The initial ionizing event interacts directly with a DNA molecule.
• Indirect Effect:
• The ionizing event interacts with water molecules and then transfers energy to DNA.
• Since the human body is made up of ~80% water and less than 1% DNA, the majority of
radiation effects in vivo result from indirect effects.
Tissues and Organs
• The principal organ systems of the body include:
• Nervous system
• Digestive system
• Endocrine system
• Respiratory system
• Reproductive system
• The radiosensitivity of tissues and organs varies widely:
• Reproductive cells: highly radiosensitive
• Nerve cells: less radiosensitive

Fundamental Principles of Radiobiology
The law of Bergonie and Tribondeau states that:
1. Stem or immature cells are more radiosensitive than mature cells.
2. Younger tissues and organs are more radiosensitive than older tissues and organs.
3. The higher the metabolic cell activity, the more radiosensitive it is.
4. The greater the proliferation and growth rate for tissues, the greater the radiosensitivity
In diagnostic imaging, the law serves to remind us that fetuses are considerably more sensitive to radiation
exposure as are children compared with the mature adults.
Linear Energy Transfer (LET)
• Definition:
Linear energy transfer (LET) is a measure of the rate at which energy is transferred from ionizing radiation
to soft tissue.
• Biological Effect:
• As LET increases, the ability of radiation to cause biologic damage also increases.
• This relationship is expressed by Relative Biologic Effectiveness (RBE).
• Relationship Between LET and RBE:
• Higher LET → Higher RBE
The RBE of diagnostic x-rays is 1.
The LET of diagnostic x-rays is approximately 3 keV/um.

Oxygen Effect and Radiosensitivity
The Oxygen Enhancement Ratio (OER) describes how biological tissue responds more strongly to radiation
when it is oxygenated (aerobic).
Deterministic Effects of Radiation
Exposure to a high dose of radiation can cause a response within days to weeks. This type of response is
called a deterministic effect.
The series of events following high-level radiation exposure
that can lead to death within days or weeks is known as
Acute Radiation Syndrome (ARS).
Acute Radiation Syndrome (ARS) occurs when the entire body
is exposed to 1 Gy or more of ionizing radiation.
These syndromes are hematologic death, gastrointestinal (GI)
death, and central nervous system (CNS) death.

The Hemopoietic system
consists of bone marrow, circulating blood, and lymphoid tissue. Lymphoid tissues are the lymph nodes,
spleen, and thymus.
The principal response of the hemopoietic system to radiation exposure is a decrease in the numbers of all
types of blood cells in the circulating peripheral blood.
After exposure, the first cells to become affected are the lymphocytes. These cells are reduced in number
(lymphopenia) - cell depletion increases with increasing dose
The lymphocytes and the spermatogonia are the most radiosensitive cells in the body.
LD50/60
• Definition: The whole-body radiation dose that causes 50% of irradiated subjects to die within 60 days.
• Example:
• LD50/30 → dose required to kill 50% of a population in 30 days.
• Humans often survive >30 days, so LD50/60 is more relevant.

Stochastic Effects (No Threshold, Probability ↑ with dose)
• No threshold → even small doses carry some risk
• Probability increases with dose (but severity is not dose-related)
• Examples:
• Leukemia
• Genetic mutations
Our radiation protection guides are based on the stochastic effects of radiation and on linear, nonthreshold
dose-response relationships.
Radiation-Induced Malignancies
• Ionizing radiation can cause cancers that may not appear until years or decades after exposure.
• Different cancers show different dose–response relationships.
Examples
1. Leukemia → Linear, non-threshold (LNT)
2. Skin carcinoma → Threshold
3. Thyroid cancer → LNT
4. Breast cancer → Linear, non-threshold
5. Osteosarcoma (bone cancer) → LNT (classically seen in radium dial painters)
6. Lung cancer → LNT (notable in uranium miners exposed to radon)

Radiation protection
Protection principles
The health physicist is a radiation scientist who is concerned with the research, teaching, or operational
aspects of radiation safety or control.
Always practice ALARA: Keep radiation exposures as low as reasonably achievable.
Health physics is concerned with providing occupational radiation protection and minimizing radiation
dose to the public.
We practice ALARA in response to our acceptance of the linear nonthreshold (LNT) radiation doseresponse relationship.
ALARA (as low as reasonably achievable) defines the principal concept of radiation protection.

CARDINAL PRINCIPLES OF RADIATION PROTECTION
Three cardinal principles of radiation protection developed for nuclear
activities
Radiation protection
1. Minimize time
2. Maximize distance ( As the distance between the source of
radiation and the person increases, radiation exposure decreases
rapidly. This decrease in exposure is calculated using the inverse
square law )
3. Use shielding ( Shielding used in diagnostic radiology usually
consists of lead, although conventional building materials also are
used )
The Radiation Safety Officer is specifically responsible for developing an appropriate radiation safety pro gram
for the facility that follows internationally accepted guidelines for radiation protection.
The RSO also must review and maintain radiation monitoring records for all personnel and be available to
provide counseling for individuals (e.g., those who receive monitor readings in excess of allowable limits).

Patient protection
Repeat Radiographs
• Often caused by poor communication between the patient and radiographer.
• Emphasis: Clear instructions reduce unnecessary repeat exposures.
The Pregnant Patient
• Use the 10-day rule → Elective radiologic examinations should be scheduled within 10 days of the onset
of menstruation to minimize risk of irradiating an early pregnancy.
Gonadal Shielding
• Should always be used if the gonads are within 5 cm of the primary X-ray beam.
Patient Immobilization Considerations
• Voluntary motion → controlled by proper communication + immobilization devices.
• Involuntary motion → managed by using short exposure times with high mA.
• Immobilization is necessary when patients cannot cooperate (children, certain adults).
Order of Preference for Holding Patients
1. First choice: Friend or relative (preferably male → cannot be pregnant).
2. Last choice: Radiographer (to avoid unnecessary occupational exposure).

Protection for Health Workers
1. Monitoring of Personnel (Dosimetry)
• Purpose: Measurement of ionizing radiation doses received by personnel (external exposure).
• Method: Wearing personnel dosimeters during working hours.
Types of Personnel Dosimeters (Diagnostic Radiology):
1. Film badge → Film emulsion: silver halide
2. Thermoluminescent dosimeter (TLD) → Crystal: lithium fluoride
3. Optically stimulated luminescence (OSL) → Crystal: aluminum oxide
4. Pocket dosimeter
• Monitoring frequency: Film badges, OSLs, and TLDs are monitored monthly.
• RSO (Radiation Safety Officer): Ensures personnel do not exceed occupational dose limits.
• Fixer solution (film processing): sodium thiosulfate.
2. Use of Protective Garments
• Material of choice: Lead (high atomic number → absorbs most scatter radiation).
• Evaluation: Shields tested using Half-Value Layer (HVL) → thickness of lead that reduces beam intensity
• by 50%.

Structural Shielding Construction
Types of Fixed Barriers
1. Primary Protective Barriers
• Purpose: Prevent direct (unscattered) radiation from
reaching personnel or the public.
• Location: Positioned perpendicular to the undeflected path
of the X-ray beam.
• Example: Walls behind the X-ray tube housing.

(Control booths are regarded as
secondary barriers)

2. Secondary Protective Barriers
• Purpose: Protect against scattered radiation and tube
leakage radiation.
• Location: Positioned parallel to the line of travel of the
primary X-ray beam.
• Examples: Control booth barrier, ceiling, walls not directly
in beam path.

MOBILE EXAM CONSIDERATIONS
The mobile unit exposure switch must permit the radiographer to obtain a distance of at least 6 feet
(2meter) from the X-ray tube, patient, and useful beam.
It is recommended that the radiographer stand at least 6 feet from the X-ray source.

FLUOROSCOPIC EXAM CONSIDERATIONS
Occupational Exposure in Fluoroscopy
• Primary source of exposure: Scatter radiation from the patient (not the X-ray tube directly).
• Best protection method:
• Distance (most effective)
• High kVp technique (second choice → reduces patient dose & scatter).
Protective Devices
• Lead apron: Minimum 0.5 mm Pb equivalent (must be worn by all personnel in the room).
• Lead gloves: At least 0.25 mm Pb equivalent (if hands must be in the primary beam).
• Thyroid shield: 0.5 mm Pb equivalent (protects the thyroid gland).
Positioning Advantage
• Radiographer may stand behind the radiologist during fluoroscopy.
• Gains extra protection from the radiologist’s lead apron.
