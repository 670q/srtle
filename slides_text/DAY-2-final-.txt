3. X-ray Physics
What is an X-ray?
X-rays are a form of electromagnetic radiation with high energy and short wavelengths, allowing them to
penetrate materials. They are similar to visible light but carry more energy, enabling them to pass through
tissues and create diagnostic images.
How are X-rays Created?
X-rays are produced in an X-ray tube when fast-moving electrons, generated at the cathode, collide with the
anode (a metal target). The kinetic energy of these electrons is transformed into X-ray photons through two
mechanisms:
1.Bremsstrahlung Radiation: When electrons are decelerated or deflected by the nucleus of the target
atom.
2.Characteristic Radiation: When an electron is ejected from the inner shell of a target atom and another
electron fills the vacancy.

3.3 INVERSE SQUARE LAW:
The intensity of electromagnetic radiation decreases as the square of the distance from its source.
The inverse square law relies on the following assumptions:
1. The radiation source is a point source.
2. Radiation travels in straight lines.
3. Radiation is emitted equally in all directions.
4. The energy is radiated at a constant rate.

To follow any updates/courses:
Group: t.me/SRTLE1

TWICE the distance = ¼ Intensity

For example, if the radiation exposure is 100 mR at 1 meter, it will be reduced to 25 mR at 2 meters,
according to the inverse square law. This means doubling the distance reduces the radiation by a factor of 4.
Increasing distance always reduces radiation exposure.
Application in Radiographic Imaging:
For portable X-ray units or fluoroscopy machines, radiologic technologists often adjust the tube-to-detector
distance to achieve optimal image brightness and contrast while applying the inverse square law to balance
dose and exposure.

4. PRODUCTION AND CONTROL OF X-RAY

4.1 OPERATING CONSOLE
The operating console is the central interface for radiologic technologists, allowing precise adjustments
to the X-ray imaging parameters. It is designed to balance patient safety, image quality, and operator
efficiency.
Key Features of the Operating Console:
1.

Control of kVp (Kilovoltage Peak):

•

Purpose: Adjusts the energy and penetrative ability of the X-ray beam.

•

Range: Typically, 40–150 kVp for diagnostic imaging.

•Higher kVp: Produces more penetrating X-rays, useful for imaging dense body parts like the chest or
abdomen. Results in reduced image contrast but greater tissue penetration.
•Lower kVp: Enhances contrast for soft tissue imaging, such as mammography or extremities.
Practical Example: A chest X-ray typically requires 110–125 kVp, while extremity imaging may use 50–60 kVp.

2.

Control of mAs (Milliampere-Seconds):

•
Purpose: Regulates the number of X-rays produced by controlling the tube current (mA) and
exposure time (seconds).
• Higher mAs: Increases the quantity of X-rays, producing darker (denser) images.
• Lower mAs: Reduces radiation exposure, ideal for thinner regions or pediatric imaging.
Practical Example: A hand X-ray may use 5 mAs, while a lumbar spine X-ray might require 60–80 mAs.

3. Selection of Focal Spot Size:
•

Small Focal Spot: (~0.6 mm) for high-resolution imaging of small structures like fingers.

•

Large Focal Spot: (~1.2 mm) for higher exposures and larger body parts like the chest.
Small focal spots range from 0.1 to 1 mm; large focal spots range from 0.3 to 2 mm.

Radiology requires small focal spots because the smaller the focal spot, the better the spatial resolution
of the image. Unfortunately, as the size of the focal spot decreases, the heating of the target is
concentrated onto a smaller area. This is the limiting factor to focal spot size.
4. Automatic Exposure Control (AEC):
• AEC devices terminate the exposure automatically once sufficient radiation reaches the detector.
Ensures consistent image quality and reduces patient dose.
• Practical Application: In chest X-rays, AEC optimizes exposure based on lung density, minimizing
overexposure.

3. Line Focus Principle: The line focus principle is a design feature of the X-ray tube that reduces the effective size of
the focal spot while maintaining a larger actual focal area on the anode. This balances heat dissipation with image

Key Components:

Actual Focal Spot:

•

Larger spots are used to dissipate heat effectively.

2.

Effective Focal Spot:

•

Smaller effective focal spots improve image sharpness (spatial resolution).

The anode is angled (commonly 5–20°) relative to the electron beam.
By tilting the anode, the effective focal spot appears smaller than the actual focal spot.

Advantages of the Line Focus Principle:
Allows flexibility in balancing heat dissipation and image quality.

5.2 QUALITY:
The penetrability of an x-ray beam is called the x-ray quality.
Penetrability refers to: the ability of x-rays to penetrate deeper in tissue.
High-energy x-rays are able to penetrate tissue more deeply than low-energy x-rays.
X-rays with high penetrability are termed high-quality x-rays. Those with low penetrability are lowquality x-rays.
