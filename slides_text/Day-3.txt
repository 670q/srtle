Section II : Patient Care
1. Radiographic Image Quality

Radiographic image quality is influenced by three major interrelated
categories:
• Film factors
• Geometric factors
• Subject factors

Image quality refers to the degree of accuracy with which the anatomical
structure being examined is represented on the radiograph.

Key Characteristics of Radiographic Image Quality

•Spatial resolution
•Contrast resolution
•Noise

Resolution The ability to visualize two distinct objects and differentiate them from one another.
1. Spatial Resolution
Definition: The ability to image fine details or small objects that exhibit high subject
contrast, such as a bone–soft tissue interface, breast microcalcifications, or a calcified
lung nodule.

2. Contrast Resolution
Definition: The ability to distinguish between anatomical structures of similar
subject contrast, such as liver–spleen or gray matter–white matter.

• Factors Affecting Contrast Resolution in Radiography:
In conventional radiography:
• Grain size of the film
• Development time
• Concentration and temperature of the developing solution
• Overall film density

3 – Noise

Image noise is the grainy appearance seen in an image.
Main source in radiography: Scatter radiation

Lower noise improves image quality by enhancing
contrast resolution

•In digital radiography: quantum noise, caused by the
random distribution of x-ray photons reaching the image
receptor, often linked to insufficient mA.
Optical density
Refers to the degree of blackening on the radiographic film, which is
determined by exposure, film processing, and film characteristics

1.2 Geometric factors
1- Magnification: The relationship between size of object and its image
Two factors affect image magnification: SID and OID.
Magnification is the ratio of image size to object size or of source-to-image
receptor distance (SID) to source-to-object distance (SOD).

2- Distortion
The object should be positioned parallel to the image receptor at a fixed OID.
If any of these conditions is changed, magnification will vary across the object .
Distortion depends on:
• Object thickness
• Object position
• Object shape

Key notes:
•Thick objects are more distorted than thin ones.
•Distortion occurs if the object plane and image plane are not parallel.

Two main types of shape distortion:
1. Elongation: Image appears longer than the true object.

Caused by improper alignment of:
• Image receptor
• X-ray tube (central ray)
2. Foreshortening: Image appears shorter than the true object.

•Caused by improper alignment of:
•Anatomical part
Key notes:

Both elongation and foreshortening result from misalignment of the
central ray (CR), anatomical part, and/or image receptor (IR).

3 – Focal-Spot Blur : Focal-spot blur is the most important
factor for determining spatial resolution.
OID Effect: Blur is minimized when the object-to-image
receptor distance (OID) is small.

1.Smaller OID → smaller focal-spot blur → better spatial
resolution.
2. Maintaining a long source-to-object distance (SOD) also
helps reduce blur.

1.3 Subject contrast
•Body habitus affects tissue thickness, density, and atomic number.
•Larger patients (hypersthenic) → greater attenuation → lower subject contrast; require
higher exposure.
•Smaller or thinner patients (asthenic/hyposthenic) → less attenuation → higher
subject contrast; require lower exposure.

Production of scatter radiation:
Two types of image-forming X-rays exit the patient:
1.X-rays that pass through tissue without interacting.
2.X-rays that are scattered in tissue by the Compton interaction and
therefore contribute only noise to the image.
THREE FACTORS that contribute to increased scatter radiation and
ultimately to image noise:
•Increasing kVp
•Increasing X-ray field size
•Increasing anatomical thickness
Three primary factors influencing the relative intensity of scatter
radiation that reaches the image receptor:
1.kVp
2.Field size
3.Patient thickness

Control of Scatter Radiation
Contrast is one of the most important characteristics of the radiographic image. Scatter
radiation, the result of Compton interaction, is the primary factor that reduces image
contrast.
Devices that reduce the amount of scatter radiation reaching the image receptor:
1. Beam restrictors
2. Grids
3. Air-Gap Technique

3.2 Digital Radiography (DR)

DAY 3

A form of X-ray imaging where the image is captured and stored digitally, eliminating
the need for traditional film or chemical processing.
Key Features:

• Immediate image availability — no darkroom or processing chemicals.
• Wide dynamic range — allows visualization of both low and high-density
structures in the same image.
• Image manipulation — brightness, contrast, and magnification can be adjusted
post-acquisition without repeat exposure.
• Lower radiation dose (in many cases) due to higher detector sensitivity.
• PACS integration — images can be stored, retrieved, and transmitted
electronically.

3.3 Two Main Types:
1.Indirect DR

Uses a scintillator (e.g., CsI, GdOS) to convert X-rays into light.
Light is then converted to an electronic signal via CCD, CMOS, or TFT.

2.Direct DR

Uses a photoconductor (e.g., a-Se) to convert X-rays directly into electrical charges.
Charges are read out by a TFT array.
Advantages Over CR & Film:
•Faster workflow.
•Higher efficiency in image capture and transfer.
•Better dose utilization.

5. Mammography Imaging

DAY 3

X-ray Tube
•Focal spot size: Fine focus of 0.2–0.5 mm; additional 0.1 mm for
magnification views.
•Target materials: Molybdenum (Mo) and Rhodium (Rh).
•Beryllium window: Minimizes X-ray absorption within the tube.
•Molybdenum filter: Allows only characteristic radiation to pass;
absorbs unwanted radiation to form a monoenergetic beam.
Orientation:
•Cathode: Positioned toward the chest wall.
•Anode: Positioned toward the nipple.
Filtration Requirements
•Minimum total beam filtration: Not less than 0.5 mm aluminum equivalent.
