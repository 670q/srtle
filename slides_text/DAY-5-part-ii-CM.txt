3. Properties and classification
Contrast Media (CM)
Special agents that can be administered to improve the visibility (contrast) of soft tissues in radiographic imaging

3.1 Characteristics of an Ideal Contrast Medium:
1. Electrically neutral (no charge)
2. Non-reactive and biologically safe
3. Contains an adequate amount of iodine atoms
4. Low osmolality
5. Low osmolarity
6. Low viscosity
7. Excellent compatibility with nervous tissue
8. Can be given through multiple routes (IV, IA, IT, or GIT)

• Viscosity
A measure of a fluid’s resistance to flow.
•High viscosity liquids are often described as thick or syrupy; low viscosity liquids are considered thin or watery.
•Warming the contrast medium to body temperature before injection can help reduce its viscosity.

• Toxicity
•The potential of a substance to cause harm to the body.

• Osmolality
The number of dissolved particles in a solution per kilogram of water.
•

Ionic media: Molecules dissociate into charged particles in solution, increasing osmolality.

•

Nonionic media: Molecules remain whole in solution, resulting in lower osmolality.

→ High-osmolality contrast agents are generally more toxic, while low-osmolality agents are less toxic and preferred
for most iodinated contrast procedures.
→ Isosmolar agents (matching blood osmolality) are used for high-risk patients to minimize adverse effects.

3.2 Contrast media Classification :
1. Neutral
•

Produces minimal change in radiographic appearance.

2. Negative (Radiolucent) – Appears dark on images
•

Examples: Gases such as air and carbon dioxide (CO₂).

•

Commonly used in studies like arthrography and myelography.

→

Carbon dioxide is safe, effective, and frequently used as a negative contrast agent.

3. Positive (Radiopaque) – Appears white or light on images

•

Examples: Barium sulfate (BaSO₄) and iodine-based compounds.

→

These agents absorb more x-rays than surrounding tissues, creating high-contrast images.

K-edge
Iodine K-edge = 33.2 keV
Barium K-edge = 37.44 KeV

Percutaneous transluminal angioplasty involves using an

4. Extravasation
intravenous (IV) fluids or medications may leak or accidentally enter the tissues surrounding a vein. This is known
as extravasation, also called infiltration.
•
Extravasation means the fluid has escaped from the vessel.
•
Infiltration refers to the fluid spreading into nearby tissue.
Precautions to prevent infiltration:
1. Always check for blood backflow to confirm the catheter is correctly positioned before injecting.
2. Secure and Immobilize the catheter at the injection site to prevent movement.
3. Stop the injection immediately if:
•
The patient feels discomfort at the site
•
You notice swelling around the site
•
You encounter resistance during injection

If infiltration occurs:
1. Remove the needle and address the issue before attempting another injection elsewhere.
2. Reassure the patient that the discomfort will be temporary.
3. Apply pressure to the vein until bleeding stops completely to prevent a hematoma from forming.
4. Once bleeding has stopped, place a cold pack on the affected area to relieve pain, constrict blood vessels,
and help keep the leaked fluid localized.
5.In the past, hot packs were advised for all IV infiltrations to increase circulation and speed fluid absorption.

5. Precautions
Consent
Procedures involving intravenous (IV), intraarterial, or intrathecal administration of iodine contrast carry inherent risks, so
obtaining informed consent is typically required.

Patient history
A thorough patient history is crucial, as allergic reactions to contrast agents can occur due to sensitivity to iodine or other
components of the contrast medium.
Renal failure
Patients with renal failure may have difficulty eliminating the contrast medium, potentially leading to toxic reactions. Such toxic
effects, which can range from mild to severe, are more likely in patients with compromised heart or kidney function or those who
receive an excessive dose of the contrast agent.
Radiographers must review the patient’s chart to confirm that blood urea nitrogen (BUN) and creatinine levels fall within normal
ranges.
The typical normal values for adults are:
For adult males: 0.74 to 1.35 mg/dL (65.4 to 119.3 mmol)
1.
BUN: 6 to 20 mg/dL
For adult females: 0.59 to 1.4 mg/dL (52.2 to 91.9 mmol)
2.
Creatinine: 0.6 to 1.5 mg/dL
Creatinine levels of 2.0 mg/dL or higher may be a contraindication for administering iodine contrast agents.
Any abnormal test results should be reported to the radiologist before giving iodinated contrast

6. Reactions

Most reactions to contrast media are considered allergic responses.
Mild reactions:
These are the most common and usually don’t require treatment.
Patients who experience mild reactions should be monitored for 30 minutes after symptoms begin to ensure the reaction does not
worsen.
Moderate reactions:
These reactions are not life-threatening but often require treatment to relieve symptoms.
Severe reactions:
These are potentially life-threatening and typically occur within the first 20 minutes after intravascular injection of contrast. Though
rare, severe reactions must be identified and treated immediately.
If a patient’s history indicates a high risk of adverse reaction:
1. A nonionic contrast agent may be preferred.
2. The procedure might be preceded by administration of antihistamines or corticosteroids to reduce the risk of reaction.
3. If the patient has an iodine allergy and has not been premedicated, the procedure may be cancelled and an alternative imaging
modality such as ultrasound or non-contrast spiral CT should be considered.

Contrast-Induced Nephropathy (CIN):

CIN is defined as acute kidney failure occurring within 48 hours after contrast injection and is a subset of post-contrast acute kidney
injury (AKI). The main risk factors include diabetes and chronic kidney disease.
Adequate hydration is critical to prevent CIN. Patients should be encouraged to drink several liters of water or fluids 12 to 24 hours
before and after receiving contrast.
For patients at risk of kidney impairment, serum creatinine and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) should be checked.
Stages of Chronic Kidney Disease based on GFR:
• Stage 1: Normal or high GFR (> 90 mL/min)
• Stage 2: Mild CKD (GFR 60-89 mL/min)
• Stage 3B: Moderate CKD (GFR 30-44 mL/min)
• Stage 4: Severe CKD (GFR 15-29 mL/min)

Intravenous hydration is recommended for all patients
with GFR below 60 mL/min receiving intra-arterial contrast.
Contrast administration should be avoided in patients with GFR less than 30 mL/min.

Nephrogenic Systemic Fibrosis (NSF) GBCA
Definition: Fibrotic disease affecting skin ± organs; linked mainly to
gadolinium exposure in severe kidney disease.
•High-Risk Groups: CKD4–5 (eGFR <30), dialysis patients, and those with
AKI; risk increases with Group I GBCAs.
•GBCA Safety: Group II = very low risk (preferred); Group I = highest risk
(avoid in high-risk patients); Group III = limited data.
•Onset & Dose: Usually appears days–months after exposure; can occur
after a single standard dose.
•Prevention: Screen for renal impairment, use lowest effective dose, choose
Group II for at-risk patients, avoid unnecessary GBCA use.

Radiographer’s Role in Suspected Shock:

Section II : Medications

Medications are substances prescribed to treat conditions by producing therapeutic effects.
Medication Properties
Pharmacokinetics VS Pharmacodynamics
• Pharmacokinetics study of the way the body processes a drug, including how it is absorbed, distributed to the site
of action, metabolized (chemically and physically changed), and eliminated.
• Pharmacodynamics is the study of the effects of drugs on the body’s normal physiological functions.
Absorption

This is the process by which a drug moves from the administration site into the bloodstream to
produce the desired effect.

Distribution

Refers to how a drug travels from the bloodstream to the target tissues where it exerts its effects.

Metabolism

The body transforms drugs into inactive forms that can be eliminated, primarily through the liver.

Excretion

The process of removing metabolized drugs from the body.

Therapeutic Effect: The intended, beneficial outcome of administering a medication.
Side Effects: Unintended effects of a drug that may or may not be harmful.
Toxic Effects: Harmful reactions often caused by overdose or impaired drug elimination.
Idiosyncratic Effect: An unpredictable or unusual reaction that differs from the expected response.
Synergistic Effect: An enhanced effect that occurs when two or more medications are taken together.
Frequently used medications encountered by radiographers include:

1. Antihistamines (such as diphenhydramine)
2. Anti-inflammatory drugs (such as Solu-Cortef) that treat allergic reactions
3. Sympathetic nervous system stimulants (such as epinephrine) to treat acute allergic reactions and other
acute conditions
4. Antimicrobials (such as alcohol) to prevent infections
5. Anticonvulsants (such as diazepam) to treat seizures
6. Antiarrhythmics (such as lidocaine or amiodarone) to treat irregular heart rhythm
7. Analgesics (such as morphine or aspirin) to relieve pain
8. Sedatives and tranquilizers (such as lorazepam or diazepam) to reduce anxiety
9. Antagonists (such as flumazenil or naloxone) to reverse the effects of sedatives
10. local anesthetics (such as lidocaine) to cause numbness when a procedure may be painful
11. Muscle relaxants (such as succinylcholine chloride) to cause temporarily paralysis
12. Hypoglycemic agents (such as insulin and metformin) to regulate blood glucose levels
13. ACE inhibitors (such as captopril, enalapril, and lisinopril) to control hypertension.

Medication Administration - Medication Orders

When medications are given in the imaging department, a physician selects the drug, determines the route of
administration, and prescribes the exact dosage.
A standing order consists of written directions for a specific medication or procedure, signed by a physician

Routes Of Administration
Enteral Route: Oral, Rectal, and Nasogastric Tube .

The oral route of administering medication is common and familiar. Oral medications are supplied in a variety of forms,
including tablets, capsules, granules, and liquids.
When a patient is severely nauseated or unable to swallow, medications can be administered by rectum

Medication Inhalation

Some lung conditions are treated with inhalation therapy. Liquid medications are vaporized, administered by an inhaler or
nebulizer, and inhaled by the patient.
The inhalation route is also used for the administration of radioactive gases for nuclear medicine lung ventilation studies

IV route

Medication administered via the IV route produces a very rapid response, which is why this route is
commonly used to administer emergency medications.
This route is also used to administer infusions for fluid and/or electrolyte replacement. Contrast agents
are administered via this route for some x-ray, CT, and MRI examinations.
When monitoring IV fluids, note the flow rate, maintain the bag or bottle at a height of 18 to 20 inches
above the vein, and notify the nursing service if the flow stops .

There are four routes for parenteral medications.

Each type of injection requires a specific skill set to ensure the medication is prepared properly and
administered into the correct location. The four types of injections are:
1. Subcutaneous (SC): This injection places medication/solution the loose connective tissue just under
the dermis.
2. Intradermal (ID): This injection places the medication into the dermis just under the epidermis.
3. Intramuscular (IM): This injection places the medication into the body of a muscle.
4. Intravenous (IV): This injection places the medication/solution into a vein through an existing IV line or a
short venous access device (saline lock). Medications given by the intravenous route can be given as an IV
bolus, as an intermittent (piggyback) medication, or in a large volume continuous infusion.
