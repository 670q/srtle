Contrast Media

Radiolucent and radiopaque contrast media are used to make the gastrointestinal (GI) tract visible on
radiographs.
Radiolucent (negative) contrast agents include swallowed air, carbon dioxide (CO₂) gas, and the natural
gas bubbles found in the stomach. Calcium and magnesium citrate carbonate crystals are often used
to generate CO₂ gas for this purpose.

Barium Sulfate

The most commonly used positive (radiopaque) contrast medium for visualizing the gastrointestinal (GI)
system is barium sulfate (BaSO₄).

IQ

Barium Types

• Thin barium has a milkshake-like consistency and is used for
imaging the entire gastrointestinal tract.
• Thick barium is harder to swallow but is ideal for examining the
esophagus, as it moves slowly and provides better coating of the
mucosal lining.

Contraindications to Barium Sulfate

Barium sulfate should not be used if there's a possibility that the mixture might leak into the peritoneal
cavity. If large amounts enter this space, it can cause serious complications such as intestinal infarcts
or peritonitis. This leakage can happen due to a perforated viscus or during surgery performed after
the radiographic exam. In such cases, a water-soluble, iodinated contrast agent should be used
instead—MD-Gastroview is one example.

Double-Contrast

Double-contrast techniques are commonly used to improve the diagnosis of various diseases and
conditions during upper GI series. Some departments also perform double-contrast esophagograms.
These procedures use both radiolucent (air or gas) and radiopaque (barium) contrast media. The doublecontrast method helps to better visualize potential polyps, diverticula, and ulcers.

Post-Examination Elimination (Definition)

One of the functions of the large intestine is to absorb water. Any barium sulfate mixture remaining in the
large intestine after an upper GI series or barium enema may become solidified. As a result, the barium
can be difficult to eliminate. Some patients may need to take a laxative after these exams to help remove
the barium sulfate. If laxatives are contraindicated, the patient should increase their fluid and fiber intake
until their stools are free of all traces of the white barium.

The technologist should avoid standing close to the table on either side of the radiologist. Instead, they
should remain behind the radiologist, outside the scatter radiation field, to reduce radiation exposure.

Two common radiographic exams of the upper gastrointestinal (GI) system that use contrast media are the
esophagogram (also known as a barium swallow) and the upper GI series. Each of these procedures is

Upper GI (UGI) Radiographic Examination
Definition and purpose
A radiographic examination of the distal esophagus, stomach, and duodenum is called an upper
gastrointestinal (upper GI or UGI) study. The purpose of the upper GI is to radiographically assess the
structure and function of the distal esophagus, stomach, and duodenum, as well as to detect any
abnormal anatomical or functional conditions.

• Colon vs Large Intestine
The large intestine and colon are often used interchangeably, but they are not exactly the same. The
colon is made up of four sections and specific bends but does not include the cecum or rectum. The
four sections of the colon are: (1) ascending colon, (2) transverse colon, (3) descending colon, and (4)
sigmoid colon. Additionally, the right (hepatic) and left (splenic) colic flexures—the bends between
these sections—are considered part of the colon.

• Small Bowel Procedures
There are four radiographic techniques used to examine the small intestine. The first two
methods are the most commonly used. The third and fourth methods are specialized
procedures, typically reserved for cases where the first two are inadequate or not
recommended.
1. Combined Upper GI and Small Bowel Series
2. Small Bowel Series Only
3. Enteroclysis
4. Intubation Method
• Contrast Media
For most small bowel studies, a thin barium sulfate suspension is used. However, if a bowel
perforation is suspected or surgery is planned soon after the exam, a water-soluble, iodinated
contrast agent is used instead. In cases where the patient has reduced bowel movement
(hypomotility), ice water or another stimulant might be administered to help move the barium
through the intestines. Additionally, iodinated contrast can be mixed with barium to enhance
peristalsis and speed up the passage of contrast through the small bowel.

Delayed Radiographs
• First method
To track the progression of barium through the entire large intestine, the radiologist may order
delayed imaging. When barium is administered orally, it typically reaches the rectum within 24
hours.
• Second method
Fluoroscopy combined with spot imaging and the use of a compression cone can help improve
visualization of the ileocecal valve.
• Third method
One disadvantage of enteroclysis is that it may cause significant patient discomfort and carries a risk
of bowel perforation during catheter insertion. This procedure is typically used for patients with
conditions such as small bowel obstruction (ileus), regional enteritis (Crohn’s disease), or
malabsorption syndromes.

• For All Method
The main goal of preparing a patient for an upper GI or small bowel series is to ensure the stomach is
empty. The patient must avoid all food and fluids for at least 8 hours before the exam. Ideally, a lowresidue diet should be followed for 48 hours prior to a small bowel study. During the fasting (NPO)
period, patients should also avoid using tobacco, nicotine products, or chewing gum. Additionally, they
should be instructed to empty their bladder before the procedure to prevent the distended bladder from
displacing the ileum.

• Barium Enema (Lower GI Series)
A barium enema is a radiographic procedure used to examine the large
intestine using contrast media.
This study helps visualize the colon and its structures. It is also referred to as
a BE (BaE) or lower GI series.
• Contraindications
There are two main contraindications for performing a barium enema, similar
to those for the small bowel series: a suspected perforated hollow organ
(viscus) and a possible large bowel obstruction. In such cases, barium
should not be used. Instead, a water-soluble contrast medium is
recommended, even though it is less radiopaque than barium sulfate.
• Appendicitis
A barium enema is generally not performed in cases of acute appendicitis due to the risk of perforation.
When the clinical diagnosis is uncertain, high-resolution ultrasound with graded compression and CT
are the preferred imaging modalities for diagnosing acute appendicitis.

Barium Enema Procedure – Patient Preparation
Preparing the patient for a barium enema is more extensive than for examinations of the stomach or small
bowel. However, the goal remains the same: the section of the alimentary canal being examined must be
completely empty. Complete cleansing of the large intestine is essential to ensure a successful and
accurate contrast media study.
Contraindications for Use of Laxatives (Cathartics)
Certain conditions prevent the use of strong laxatives or purgatives that are typically used to cleanse the
large bowel. These contraindications include:
Gross rectal bleeding, Severe diarrhea, Bowel obstruction, Inflammatory conditions such as appendicitis. A
laxative is a substance that stimulates frequent, soft, or liquid bowel movements. It works by increasing
peristalsis in the large intestine—and sometimes in the small intestine—by irritating the sensory nerve
endings in the intestinal lining. This action speeds up the movement of contents through the digestive tract.
Types of Laxatives
There are two main classes of laxatives that may be prescribed:
• Irritant Laxatives – e.g., castor oil; these stimulate the intestinal lining to increase peristalsis.
• Saline Laxatives – e.g., magnesium citrate or magnesium sulfate; these draw water into the intestines
to soften stool and promote bowel movement.

Summary Of Enema Tip Insertion Producer
Step 1: Explain the enema tip insertion procedure to the patient and answer any questions.
Step 2: Position the patient in the Sims position—lying on the left side with the right knee
and hip flexed.
Step 3: Shake the enema bag again to ensure the barium sulfate suspension is properly
mixed. Let barium flow through the tubing and out of the tip to eliminate any air.
Step 4: While wearing gloves, coat the enema tip generously with water-soluble lubricant.
Step 5: On expiration, insert the tip toward the umbilicus approximately 2.5 to 4 cm (1 to 1½
inches).
Step 6: After initial insertion, advance the tip superiorly and slightly anteriorly. Do not
exceed 7.5 to 10 cm (3 to 4 inches) total insertion. Do not force the enema tip.
Step 7: Tape the tubing in place to prevent slippage. Do not inflate the retention tip unless
directed by the radiologist.
Step 8: Make sure the IV pole/enema bag is no more than 60 cm (24 inches) above the table.
Ensure the stopcock is closed, preventing barium from flowing into the patient.

Choosing a Vein
For most intravenous (IV) urogram procedures, veins
located in the antecubital fossa (the front of the elbow)
are preferred. These veins are usually large, easy to find,
and strong enough to handle a rapid injection of contrast
dye without the risk of leaking into the surrounding tissue.
Common veins used in this area for inserting the needle
include the median cubital, cephalic, and basilic veins.

General Patient Preparation for IVU
Before an intravenous urogram (IVU), patients should follow these steps:
1. Eat a light meal the evening before the procedure.
2. Take a bowel-cleansing laxative.
3. Do not eat or drink (NPO) for at least 8 hours after midnight.
4. Use an enema on the morning of the exam.
Before starting the exam, the patient should remove all clothing except shoes and socks and wear a shortsleeved hospital gown that ties in the back.
The patient should empty their bladder just before the procedure for two reasons:
1. A bladder that is too full could rupture, especially if compression is applied early during the exam.
2. Urine already in the bladder can dilute the contrast medium that collects there.
Ureteric Compression
Ureteric compression is a technique used to improve filling of the pelvicalyceal system and the upper
parts of the ureters. It also helps the kidney’s collecting system hold the contrast medium longer,
allowing for a more thorough examination.

Alternative Trendelenburg Position
The Trendelenburg position, where the head of the table is lowered about 15°, achieves similar effects as
ureteric compression but with less risk for patients who cannot tolerate compression due to their
symptoms.
Contraindications to Ureteric Compression
Ureteric compression should not be used in certain situations, including:
1. Possible ureteric stones, since it’s hard to tell if changes are from compression or a stone.
2. Abdominal masses, which can look similar to the effects of compression on images.
3. Abdominal aortic aneurysm, because the compression device might cause it to leak or rupture.
4. Recent abdominal surgery.
5. Severe abdominal pain.
6. Acute abdominal trauma.
